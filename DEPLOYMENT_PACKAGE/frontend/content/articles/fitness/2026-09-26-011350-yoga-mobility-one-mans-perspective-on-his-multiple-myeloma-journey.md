# Movement as Medicine: What One Man's Multiple Myeloma Journey Teaches Us About Yoga, Mobility, and Hope

When we think about cancer treatment, we tend to picture chemotherapy infusions, hospital corridors, and fatigue that flattens entire weeks. What we rarely picture is a yoga mat unrolled in a living room, a patient carefully moving through a gentle spinal sequence, or a man in his sixties rebuilding balance and strength one breath at a time.

But that image is increasingly backed by science. One patient's journey with multiple myeloma — a cancer of plasma cells that affects the bone marrow — offers a powerful lens for anyone interested in how movement, mobility, and mindset shape health outcomes. His story is not a cure narrative. It's a *living well* narrative. And that distinction matters enormously for fitness enthusiasts, beginners, and anyone navigating a serious diagnosis.

This article explores what multiple myeloma is, how yoga and mobility work can support people living with blood cancers and other chronic conditions, and what practical takeaways you can apply — whether you're a seasoned athlete or someone who hasn't touched a mat in years.

---

## Understanding Multiple Myeloma: A Quick Primer

Multiple myeloma is a blood cancer that develops in plasma cells, the immune system's antibody-producing factories. When these cells turn cancerous, they multiply in the bone marrow and crowd out healthy blood cells. The result is a constellation of problems: bone pain and fractures, anemia, fatigue, kidney damage, and a compromised immune system.

According to the American Cancer Society, an estimated **35,000+ new cases** are diagnosed in the United States each year, with a median age at diagnosis around **69**. It remains an incurable but increasingly *manageable* disease. Thanks to advances in targeted therapies, immunomodulatory drugs, and stem cell transplantation, many patients now live well over a decade after diagnosis — a dramatic improvement compared with survival rates just 20 years ago.

That extended survival window is exactly why quality-of-life interventions like exercise and mobility training have moved from "nice to have" to clinically relevant.

---

## Why Movement Matters in Blood Cancer Care

For decades, cancer patients were told to rest. That advice has been thoroughly overturned.

A growing body of research — including landmark studies published in *JAMA Oncology* and *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* — shows that **structured exercise during and after cancer treatment** is associated with:

- Reduced cancer-related fatigue (one of the most debilitating symptoms)
- Improved physical function and quality of life
- Better mood and lower rates of anxiety and depression
- Reduced risk of treatment-related bone loss
- Improved sleep quality

A 2022 meta-analysis in *Supportive Care in Cancer* found that yoga specifically reduced fatigue and improved sleep quality in cancer patients, with effects comparable to other moderate-intensity exercise interventions. For myeloma patients, whose bones can be fragile from lytic lesions, *low-impact, controlled movement* is often the safest and most sustainable entry point.

### Safety First: A Critical Caveat

Before we go further, an essential safety note. Multiple myeloma can cause **bone lesions and osteoporosis**, which raise fracture risk. Anyone with myeloma — or any condition affecting bone integrity — should get **explicit clearance from their oncologist and, ideally, a physical therapist or cancer rehab specialist** before starting or modifying an exercise program.

Avoid: deep spinal twists under load, high-impact jumping, heavy axial loading (barbell back squats, heavy deadlifts), and any movement that causes sharp bone pain.

Embrace: gentle range-of-motion work, supported poses, walking, resistance bands, and pool-based movement.

## Yoga and Mobility: What the Science Says

Yoga is not a cure for cancer. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. But as a *supportive* practice, it has real, measurable value.

### 1. It Addresses Fatigue Without Overloading the System

Cancer-related fatigue isn't ordinary tiredness — it's a persistent, whole-body exhaustion that rest doesn't fix. Gentle yoga, particularly restorative and yin styles, has been shown to *reduce* fatigue rather than worsen it. The key is dose: short sessions (20–40 minutes), low intensity, and permission to stop.

### 2. It Supports Bone and Joint Health — Carefully

Weight-bearing movement signals bone to remodel. But in myeloma, we need *low-impact* loading. Standing poses with light support (chair-assisted warrior, supported tree pose) provide gentle axial load without the fracture risk of jumping or heavy lifting.

### 3. It Improves Balance and Fall Prevention

Neuropathy — nerve damage causing numbness or tingling in the feet — is a common side effect of myeloma treatments like bortezomib and thalidomide. Balance work becomes a safety imperative. Single-leg stands (with a wall or chair nearby), heel-to-toe walking, and slow weight shifts are gold.

### 4. It Regulates the Nervous System

Slow breathing — particularly extended exhales — activates the parasympathetic branch of the nervous system. For patients dealing with scanxiety, treatment side effects, and uncertainty, this is not woo-woo. It's physiology. A 2023 study in *Frontiers in Psychology* found that slow, controlled breathing reduced cortisol and improved emotional regulation.

## A Practical Yoga and Mobility Framework for Chronic Illness

Here's how to structure movement if you're living with myeloma, another blood cancer, or a chronic condition that affects your energy and bones. This framework also works beautifully for deconditioned beginners.

### The Weekly Template (Adjust Based on Energy)

| Day | Focus | Duration |
|-----|-------|----------|
| Monday | Gentle mobility flow + breathwork | 20–30 min |
| Tuesday | Walking or stationary bike (low impact) | 15–30 min |
| Wednesday | Restorative yoga (supported poses) | 20–30 min |
| Thursday | Resistance bands — upper body + core | 20 min |
| Friday | Balance and gait drills + light walking | 15–20 min |
| Saturday | Chair yoga or pool session | 20–30 min |
| Sunday | Full rest or gentle stretching | — |

**Beginner adjustment:** Start with 10 minutes, three days per week. Add five minutes only when you finish feeling *better*, not wiped out.

**Advanced adjustment:** If you're further out from treatment and cleared for more, add light dumbbell work (2–8 kg), longer walks, and more challenging balance sequences. Never chase intensity at the expense of bone safety.

### Key Poses and Why They Work

- **Cat-Cow (Marjaryasana-Bitilasana):** Spinal mobility without loading. Move slowly, sync with breath.
- **Supported Bridge (Setu Bandhasana with a block):** Gentle hip and spine extension; avoid if you have spinal lesions.
- **Chair Warrior II:** Builds leg strength and stability with full support.
- **Legs Up the Wall (Viparita Karani):** Restorative; supports circulation and nervous system downregulation.
- **Seated Spinal Twist (gentle):** Aids digestion and mobility — but keep the twist shallow and never force it.

**Form cue:** In every pose, prioritize *length over depth*. If you can't breathe smoothly, you've gone too far.

## Nutrition Considerations That Pair With Movement

Exercise and nutrition work together. For myeloma patients and anyone managing chronic illness, a few evidence-based priorities stand out.

1. **Protein for muscle preservation.** Aim for **1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight daily** if kidney function allows. (Myeloma can affect kidneys, so this must be individualized with your care team.) Spread it across meals — Greek yogurt, eggs, fish, legumes, tofu.

2. **Calcium and vitamin D for bone health.** Target **1,000–1,200 mg calcium** and **600–800 IU vitamin D** daily, ideally from food first. Discuss supplementation with your oncologist, as some patients need higher D doses.

3. **Hydration.** Treatment and medications increase dehydration risk. Aim for pale-yellow urine as a simple gauge.

4. **Anti-inflammatory pattern.** A Mediterranean-style diet — olive oil, fatty fish, vegetables, whole grains, nuts — is associated with better outcomes and lower inflammation across cancer populations.

5. **Avoid extreme restriction.** During treatment, unintentional weight loss is a red flag. Prioritize adequate calories over "clean eating" perfection.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

- **Sleep 7–9 hours.** This is when tissue repair and immune function peak.
- **Deload every 4th week.** Reduce volume by 30–40% to allow adaptation.
- **Never train through bone pain.** Muscle soreness is one thing; deep, localized bone pain is a stop signal.
- **Monitor neuropathy.** If balance feels off, use a chair or wall for every standing pose.
- **Track energy, not just reps.** A simple 1–10 fatigue scale before and after sessions helps you calibrate.
- **Work with a cancer exercise specialist** (look for ACSM or CETI certification) when possible.

## Realistic Timelines: What Progress Actually Looks Like

Let's be honest about timelines, because unrealistic expectations derail people.

- **Weeks 1–4:** Improved mood and sleep; movements feel awkward. This is normal.
- **Weeks 4–8:** Noticeable energy improvements, better balance, less stiffness.
- **Months 3–6:** Measurable strength gains (if cleared for resistance work), improved endurance, better daily function.
- **Months 6–12:** Meaningful quality-of-life changes — easier stairs, less fatigue, more confidence.

Progress is rarely linear. Treatment cycles, infections, and fatigue will create dips. The goal is *consistency over intensity*, not perfection.

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

No gym? No problem.

- **No mat:** Use a folded blanket or carpet.
- **No blocks:** Stack sturdy books or use a firm cushion.
- **No bands:** Use a towel for stretching; water bottles or canned goods for light resistance.
- **Limited space:** Chair yoga and seated mobility flows require only a few square feet.
- **Pool access:** Aquatic yoga and walking are among the safest options for fragile bones.
- **Cold or fatigued:** A 10-minute bedside mobility routine still counts.

## Coaching Wisdom: What Actually Helps People Stick With It

After years of working with clients navigating illness and deconditioning, a few truths hold:

1. **Identity beats intensity.** People who say "I'm someone who moves" outlast those chasing PRs.
2. **Ritual beats motivation.** Same time, same corner of the room, same playlist.
3. **Permission beats pressure.** A 5-minute session you actually do beats the 60-minute one you skip.
4. **Community beats solo.** A class, a partner, or an online group dramatically improves adherence.
5. **Hope is a practice.** It's built in small, repeated actions — not in grand declarations.

## The Bottom Line

One man's myeloma journey isn't just a medical story. It's a reminder that *how we move through illness* — literally and figuratively — shapes how we live. Yoga and mobility work won't cure blood cancer. But they can reduce fatigue, protect balance, preserve strength, calm the nervous system, and restore a sense of agency when so much feels out of control.

Whether you're a myeloma patient, a cancer survivor, a beginner intimidated by the mat, or a seasoned athlete humbled by injury, the principle is the same: **meet your body where it is, move within safety, and show up again tomorrow.**

That's not just fitness advice. That's a philosophy for hard seasons.

*Always consult your oncologist, physician, or a certified cancer exercise specialist before beginning or changing an exercise program, especially if you have bone lesions, neuropathy, or are mid-treatment.*
