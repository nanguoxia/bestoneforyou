# The New Hobby That's Kicking My Ass: What Pickleball Taught Me About Cardio, Recovery, and Eating for Performance

The fastest-growing racquet sport in America may also be the cardio workout I've been avoiding. And it turns out, my body—and my nutrition—had a lot to say about it.

I came to pickleball the way most people do: skeptically. It looked like tennis for people who didn't want to run, a gentle pastime for retirees with a competitive streak. Then I played three games in one afternoon, woke up the next morning with sore hips, a stiff lower back, and a resting heart rate that suggested I'd sprinted a 5K. My "easy" hobby had exposed a fitness gap I'd been ignoring for years.

If you've recently picked up a new sport and found it humbling, you're in good company. Here's what the science says about why this happens, and—more importantly—how to fuel, train, and recover so your new obsession doesn't sideline you.

---

## Why Pickleball Is Harder Than It Looks

Pickleball is often described as low-impact, and that's partly true—there's less running than tennis. But the sport demands repeated short bursts of lateral movement, rapid direction changes, and sustained low crouches. A 2023 review published in *Sports Health* found that pickleball participation can elevate heart rate to moderate-to-vigorous intensities, with players frequently spending a significant portion of game time above 70% of their maximum heart rate.

Translation: it's interval training in disguise. And if your cardiovascular base is thin, your body will let you know—usually the next day.

There's also a real injury profile to respect. Research presented at the 2023 American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons annual meeting reported that pickleball injuries have risen sharply alongside the sport's popularity, with strains, sprains, and tendinopathies of the lower leg, hip, and shoulder among the most common. Most are overuse or poor-preparation injuries—which means most are preventable.

## The Nutrition Side: You Can't Out-Play a Poorly Fueled Body

Here's where my new hobby really kicked my ass—and where nutrition became the fix.

**Before you play.** A common mistake among recreational players is showing up on an empty stomach after a workday. For sessions under 60 minutes, a small snack 60–90 minutes prior works well: a banana with a tablespoon of nut butter, a small bowl of oatmeal, or a piece of toast with hummus. For longer sessions or tournament play, aim for 30–60 grams of carbohydrates plus 15–20 grams of protein in that pre-game window. A 2021 position stand from the International Society of Sports Nutrition supports carbohydrate availability as the primary driver of sustained performance in intermittent sports like this.

**During play.** For games under an hour, water is usually sufficient. Beyond that—or in hot conditions—add electrolytes. A practical coaching cue: sip 4–8 ounces of fluid every 15–20 minutes rather than chugging between games.

**After play.** This is where most recreational athletes under-deliver. Aim for 20–30 grams of protein within a couple of hours, paired with carbohydrates to replenish glycogen. Chocolate milk, Greek yogurt with fruit, or a chicken-and-rice bowl all fit the bill. A 2022 *Nutrients* review on post-exercise recovery emphasizes that protein timing and total daily intake matter more than any single "magic window"—so build the habit, don't obsess over the clock.

**Daily foundation.** If you're playing three or more times a week, your baseline matters more than any single meal. Prioritize 1.4–2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily, adequate carbohydrates on training days, and consistent hydration. Beginners often underestimate how much carbohydrate a repeated-sprint sport demands.

## Training Advice by Fitness Level

**Beginner.** Don't jump into three-hour sessions. Start with 30–45 minutes, twice weekly, and add 10 minutes per session weekly. Your first priority is tissue tolerance, not winning. Include two short strength sessions weekly focused on the basics: squats, hip hinges (like Romanian deadlifts or glute bridges), calf raises, and banded shoulder work.

**Intermediate.** Add lateral movement drills—lateral shuffles, carioca, and split-step practice—two to three times weekly for 10–15 minutes. These directly transfer to court movement and build the hip and ankle resilience the sport demands.

**Advanced.** Periodize. Rotate heavier strength blocks with lighter, more explosive work. Plyometrics—box jumps, lateral bounds—can improve first-step quickness, but introduce them gradually and only after a solid strength base.

**No equipment? No problem.** Bodyweight circuits (squats, reverse lunges, push-up variations, glute bridges, calf raises) and resistance bands cover nearly everything you need. A single set of bands is the most cost-effective pickleball investment you can make.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

- **Warm up dynamically.** Five to ten minutes of leg swings, walking lunges, banded shoulder rotations, and light court movement before play.
- **Cool down and stretch.** Focus on hip flexors, calves, and the posterior shoulder chain—the areas this sport loads most.
- **Respect the 48-hour rule.** Beginners should avoid back-to-back hard sessions early on. Tissue adaptation takes time; soreness that lingers beyond 72 hours is a signal to back off.
- **Sleep is training.** Aim for 7–9 hours. A 2021 review in *Sports Medicine* linked sleep restriction to impaired skill learning and elevated injury risk—both directly relevant to a sport built on reaction time and quick feet.
- **Watch the shoulder.** Repetitive serving and overheads stress the rotator cuff. If you feel pinching, reduce volume and add external rotation exercises with a band.

---

## Realistic Timelines

- **2–4 weeks:** Soreness decreases as your body adapts to new movement patterns.
- **6–8 weeks:** Noticeable improvement in court movement and recovery between games, assuming consistent play and basic strength work.
- **3–6 months:** Meaningful gains in endurance and skill, especially with structured practice.
- **Ongoing:** Strength and mobility work compounds—players who train off-court typically stay injury-free longer.

---

## The Bottom Line

My new hobby humbled me, and honestly, that's the point. Pickleball is a genuinely effective cardio stimulus wrapped in a social, low-barrier package—but it's still a sport, and sports reward preparation. Fuel before you play, recover after, build a modest strength base, and progress your court time gradually.

The best part? You don't have to be fit to start. You just have to be smart about how you start—and what you eat along the way.
