# Strength Training as a Tool for Mental Health: What a Former College Athlete's Depression Journey Teaches Us

When a former college athlete describes spending years "trying to power through" depression—cycling through several traditional oral antidepressants before finding one that worked—it resonates with a growing number of fitness enthusiasts who treat the gym as both a sanctuary and, sometimes, a hiding place. The story raises an important question for anyone who trains: how do we separate athletic grit from the medical care that depression actually requires?

This article unpacks what exercise science says about training and mental health, where the "push through it" mindset helps and where it can hurt, and how to build a sustainable routine that supports—rather than substitutes for—professional treatment.

## The Evidence: Exercise Helps, But It Isn't a Cure

The research on movement and mood is genuinely encouraging. A 2023 meta-analysis published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, pooling data from more than 1,000 randomized controlled trials, found that physical activity was associated with meaningful reductions in symptoms of depression and anxiety across both clinical and general populations. Resistance training and aerobic exercise both showed benefits, with moderate-to-vigorous intensities producing the largest effects.

Additional data points worth knowing:

- **Dose matters.** A landmark 2018 study in *The American Journal of Psychiatry* (over 1.2 million U.S. adults) found that people who exercised had roughly 43% fewer days of poor mental health per month than those who didn't—but more than 3–5 sessions per week or sessions beyond 45–60 minutes showed diminishing or even negative returns.
- **Consistency beats intensity.** Observational research suggests regular moderate activity is more protective than occasional heroic efforts.
- **Exercise is an adjunct, not a replacement.** Major clinical guidelines, including those from the American Psychiatric Association, position exercise as a complementary strategy alongside therapy and/or medication—not a substitute for them.

The former athlete's experience—needing to trial multiple medications before finding one that worked—is common and clinically expected. Antidepressant response varies widely between individuals, and finding the right fit often takes time and medical supervision. No training program changes that reality.

## Where the "Power Through" Mindset Helps—and Hurts

Athletes are trained to interpret discomfort as a signal to push harder. That works for interval sets. It can backfire badly with depression.

**Where it helps:**
- Establishing a non-negotiable movement habit, even at low intensity, when motivation is absent
- Using scheduled training as behavioral activation—a well-established psychological technique for depression
- Leveraging the structure and social connection of team or group training

**Where it hurts:**
- Using training to avoid seeking professional help
- Interpreting persistent low mood, sleep disruption, or loss of interest as "just needing a harder workout"
- Overtraining, which can mimic and worsen depressive symptoms (sleep disturbance, irritability, performance decline)

**Coaching perspective:** The most useful mental shift is treating a workout as a *minimum viable action* rather than a performance test. On low days, the goal is showing up—not hitting a PR.

## Practical Training Advice by Fitness Level

### Beginners
- **Start with 2–3 sessions per week** of 20–30 minutes: brisk walking, bodyweight circuits, or light resistance training.
- **Use the "10-minute rule":** commit to 10 minutes; if you still feel awful, stop without guilt. Most days, you'll continue.
- **Form cues for the basics:** For bodyweight squats—feet shoulder-width, chest tall, knees tracking over toes, sit back as if toward a chair. For push-ups—hands slightly wider than shoulders, body in one straight line, lower until elbows reach about 90 degrees (perform against a wall or elevated surface if needed).
- **Equipment-free option:** A 15-minute circuit of squats, incline push-ups, glute bridges, and dead bugs requires nothing but floor space.

### Intermediate
- **Aim for 3–4 sessions weekly**, mixing 2 resistance sessions with 1–2 aerobic sessions (150 minutes of moderate cardio per week is the standard public-health target).
- **Sample structure:** 30–40 minutes resistance training (e.g., goblet squats, dumbbell rows, Romanian deadlifts) plus a 20-minute walk or cycle.
- **Home alternative:** Resistance bands can replicate most dumbbell movements; a loaded backpack works for beginners' squats and rows.

### Advanced
- **Keep hard training hard, but cap volume during low periods.** Research on overtraining suggests monitoring sleep, resting heart rate, and mood as early-warning signs.
- **Deload every 4–6 weeks** (reduce volume by 40–50%)—especially valuable during stressful life periods.
- **Consider separating high-intensity work from high-stress weeks.** Save maximal-effort sessions for weeks when sleep and mood are stable.

## Nutrition Notes That Support Both Body and Mind

- **Protein:** Roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight daily supports muscle repair across training levels.
- **Carbohydrates:** Don't fear them—adequate carbs (3–5 g/kg for moderate training) support training quality and serotonin pathways.
- **Omega-3s:** Some evidence links higher intake (fatty fish 2–3 times weekly, or supplemental EPA/DHA) with modest mood benefits; discuss supplements with a clinician.
- **Caffeine timing:** Avoid caffeine within 8 hours of bedtime—sleep disruption is a known driver of low mood.
- **Alcohol:** Even moderate drinking can interfere with sleep architecture and antidepressant efficacy; consider tracking intake honestly.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

- **Sleep is the highest-leverage recovery tool.** Aim for 7–9 hours; protect a consistent wake time.
- **Warm-ups:** 5–10 minutes of dynamic movement (leg swings, arm circles, light cardio) before training; static stretching belongs after.
- **Listen to warning signs:** Persistent joint pain, unexplained fatigue, or a plummeting mood alongside training load warrants a step back—and a conversation with a professional.
- **Rest days are training days.** Schedule at least one full rest day weekly and treat it as part of the program, not a failure.

## Realistic Timelines

- **Mood benefits from a single session** can appear within hours (the "exercise high"), but durable effects typically build over **4–8 weeks of consistent activity**.
- **Strength gains:** Beginners often see noticeable improvements in 4–6 weeks; advanced lifters measure progress in months.
- **Medication response:** Clinical guidance generally allows 4–6 weeks for a fair trial of an antidepressant—patience and medical follow-up matter as much as consistency in the gym.

## The Bottom Line

The former athlete's story isn't a story about willpower—it's a story about persistence in finding the right support. Training can be a powerful ally in mental health: it builds structure, connection, and physiological resilience. But it works best alongside professional care, honest self-monitoring, and a willingness to adjust the plan when life demands it. Show up, scale appropriately, and ask for help when you need it. That's not weakness—it's smart programming.

*If you or someone you know is struggling with depression, seek support from a licensed healthcare provider. In the U.S., you can call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.*
