# One Man’s Perspective on His Multiple Myeloma Journey: Strength, Movement, and Hope

*Life with this blood cancer is rarely straightforward, but as one patient found, there’s a lot of hope for the future — and a surprising amount of it comes down to how you move, eat, and recover.*

---

## A Diagnosis That Rewrites the Rulebook

When David, a former recreational runner in his late fifties, was diagnosed with multiple myeloma, his first thought wasn’t about cancer — it was about whether he’d ever run again. Multiple myeloma is a cancer of plasma cells, the immune cells that live in bone marrow. It affects roughly **35,000 Americans each year**, according to the American Cancer Society, and it’s about **1.8 times more common in men than women**. It also disproportionately affects Black Americans, who face roughly **twice the incidence rate** of white Americans.

What makes myeloma uniquely challenging for fitness enthusiasts is that it attacks the very scaffolding of movement: the bones. Myeloma cells trigger lesions that weaken bone structure, and the disease can cause anemia, kidney damage, and fatigue that makes a morning workout feel like a marathon.

But here’s the part that rarely makes headlines: **well-structured exercise and nutrition are now recognized as legitimate supportive therapies** in myeloma care — not just nice-to-haves. David’s story isn’t about a miracle cure. It’s about a man who rebuilt his body, one careful rep at a time.

---

## Why This Matters to Every Fitness Enthusiast

You may be reading this without a cancer diagnosis — and that’s exactly the point. Myeloma treatment increasingly mirrors principles that apply to all of us: **load management, bone health, progressive overload, protein timing, and recovery.** The lessons from David’s journey translate directly to anyone chasing strength, longevity, or a comeback from injury.

Recent exercise science backs this up. A 2023 systematic review in *Supportive Care in Cancer* found that **supervised resistance and aerobic training in myeloma patients improved fatigue, physical function, and quality of life**, with no significant increase in adverse events when programs were properly individualized. Another study in *Blood Cancer Journal* noted that **exercise may even modulate inflammatory markers** associated with disease progression — an area of active research, not yet a proven treatment, but promising.

---

## The Foundation: Bone Health Comes First

Before any barbell touches your hands in a myeloma context — or any context where bone density is compromised — you need medical clearance and, ideally, a bone scan. Myeloma lesions can turn a routine deadlift into a fracture risk.

**Actionable advice for beginners and beyond:**

- **Get a DEXA scan and skeletal survey.** Know your bone density and lesion locations before loading.
- **Prioritize weight-bearing and impact-free work.** Walking, stationary cycling, and resistance bands are safer starting points than heavy axial loading.
- **Avoid spinal flexion under load** (think crunches with weight, good mornings, heavy deadlifts) if lesions are present in the spine or ribs.
- **Use machines before free weights** if balance or bone integrity is a concern. Leg press, chest press, and seated rows offer stability and controlled range of motion.

**Form cue:** Keep the spine neutral, brace the core as if bracing for a light punch, and exhale on exertion. Never sacrifice positioning for load.

---

## Building Strength Without Breaking Down

David started with **two sessions per week, 20 minutes each**, using resistance bands and a recumbent bike. Within three months, he progressed to light dumbbells. Within six, he was doing modified push-ups and goblet squats with a 15-pound kettlebell.

This mirrors evidence-based guidelines: the **American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM)** recommends **2–3 resistance sessions per week** for cancer survivors, targeting all major muscle groups with **1–3 sets of 8–12 reps** at moderate intensity (RPE 5–7 out of 10).

**Progression timeline (realistic):**

| Phase | Duration | Focus | Example |
|-------|----------|-------|---------|
| Foundation | Weeks 1–4 | Mobility, bands, walking | Band rows, sit-to-stands, 10-min walks |
| Build | Weeks 5–12 | Light dumbbells, machines | Goblet squats, seated press, 20-min bike |
| Strengthen | Months 4–6 | Progressive overload | Modified push-ups, lunges, 2–3 sets |
| Maintain/Advance | Month 6+ | Periodization, variety | Supersets, interval walking, yoga |

**For advanced athletes:** If you’re returning from treatment with a solid bone profile, you can reintroduce heavier loads gradually — but **increase by no more than 5–10% per week** and monitor for bone pain, which is a red flag to stop and consult your oncologist.

## Nutrition: Fueling Repair and Resilience

Myeloma and its treatments (steroids, immunomodulators, chemotherapy) can wreak havoc on appetite, muscle mass, and bone density. Nutrition becomes a strategic tool.

**Evidence-based priorities:**

- **Protein:** Aim for **1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight daily** to combat muscle wasting. A 2022 review in *Nutrients* emphasized higher protein needs in cancer patients to preserve lean mass. Spread intake across 3–4 meals, with 20–30 g per sitting.
- **Calcium and Vitamin D:** Essential for bone health. Target **1,000–1,200 mg calcium** (from food first: dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens) and **600–800 IU vitamin D** daily, adjusted per bloodwork.
- **Anti-inflammatory fats:** Omega-3s from fatty fish, walnuts, and flaxseed may help manage inflammation. Aim for **2 servings of fatty fish per week** or discuss supplementation with your care team.
- **Hydration:** Kidney health is critical in myeloma. Aim for **2–3 liters of water daily** unless fluid-restricted.

**Practical tip from coaching:** When appetite is low, prioritize protein and calories over “perfect” meals. A smoothie with Greek yogurt, banana, peanut butter, and oat milk can deliver 30 g protein and 400 calories in minutes.

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

David credits three habits for keeping him injury-free: **sleep, deload weeks, and listening to his body.**

- **Sleep:** Aim for **7–9 hours**. Poor sleep impairs recovery and immune function. A 2021 study in *Sleep Medicine Reviews* linked sleep quality to better outcomes in cancer patients.
- **Deload:** Every 4–6 weeks, reduce volume by 40–50%. This isn’t laziness — it’s adaptation.
- **Pain monitoring:** Distinguish between muscle soreness (diffuse, symmetrical, improves with movement) and bone pain (sharp, localized, worse at night). The latter warrants medical attention.
- **Avoid falls:** Balance work (single-leg stands, tai chi) is underrated. A fall can be catastrophic with compromised bones.

**Alternatives for different environments:**

- **No gym?** Use resistance bands, water bottles, and bodyweight progressions.
- **No equipment?** Chair squats, wall push-ups, and stair walking are effective.
- **Outdoors?** Walking, swimming, and cycling are joint-friendly and mood-boosting.

## The Mental Game: Hope as a Training Partner

David’s perspective is refreshingly unsentimental: “Some days I felt like a patient. Other days I felt like an athlete. The athlete days were always better.”

Research supports this. A 2020 study in *Psycho-Oncology* found that **physical activity is associated with reduced depression and anxiety** in cancer survivors. The mechanism isn’t just physiological — it’s the sense of agency, routine, and progress.

**Actionable mindset tips:**

- **Set process goals, not outcome goals.** “Walk 15 minutes today” beats “get stronger” on tough days.
- **Track something.** A simple log of workouts, sleep, and mood reveals patterns and wins.
- **Find your community.** Whether it’s a cancer rehab program, an online support group, or a training partner, isolation is the enemy of consistency.

---

## The Bottom Line

Multiple myeloma is a serious, complex disease — and this article is not medical advice. Anyone navigating it should work closely with an oncologist, physical therapist, and registered dietitian.

But David’s story carries a universal truth for fitness enthusiasts of every level: **the body responds to thoughtful movement, quality nutrition, and consistent recovery.** Whether you’re a beginner taking your first walk or an advanced lifter adjusting to new limits, the principles hold. Start where you are. Progress gradually. Respect the signals. And never underestimate the power of showing up.

**Hope, it turns out, has a training plan.**

*Always consult your healthcare provider before beginning or modifying an exercise or nutrition program, especially if you have a chronic illness or are undergoing treatment.*
