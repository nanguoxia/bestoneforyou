# The Real Secret to Ultrarunning Success: It's Not Just the Carbs

**High-carb fueling is making headlines in ultrarunning, but the science—and the athletes—tell a more nuanced story. Here's what actually matters for going the distance.**

---

## The Headline That Got Everyone Talking

When David Roche shattered the course record at the 2024 Leadville Trail 100, finishing in 15 hours, 26 minutes, and 34 seconds—nearly 15 minutes faster than the previous mark—the running world wanted to know his secret. His answer surprised many: an aggressive, high-carbohydrate fueling strategy that pushed his intake well beyond conventional endurance guidelines.

Roche reported consuming roughly 120 grams of carbohydrate per hour during portions of the race—double or even triple what sports nutrition textbooks recommended just a decade ago. Suddenly, every ultrarunner with a hydration vest was asking the same question: *Should I be eating way more carbs too?*

The short answer is: maybe—but the longer answer is where the real value lies. Carbohydrate intake is only one variable in a complex equation that includes training adaptation, gut tolerance, sleep, recovery, pacing, and individual physiology. Understanding how these pieces fit together is what separates a successful race from a sufferfest.

---

## What the Science Actually Says About Carb Intake

### The Old Guidelines Were Conservative

For years, the American College of Sports Medicine recommended 30–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour for endurance events lasting 1–2.5 hours, and up to 90 grams per hour for longer efforts. These recommendations weren't wrong—they were simply based on the limits of what researchers believed the gut could absorb.

### The New Frontier: Multiple Transportable Carbohydrates

The game changed when researchers discovered that the small intestine absorbs glucose and fructose through *different* transport mechanisms. By combining these sugars, athletes can increase total carbohydrate absorption beyond the traditional 60-gram-per-hour ceiling for glucose alone.

Studies now demonstrate that trained athletes can tolerate and oxidize 90–120 grams per hour—and in some cases up to 144 grams per hour—when using multiple transportable carbohydrate blends and when the gut is progressively trained to handle the load.

### But Here's the Catch

More carbs do not automatically equal better performance. The research shows benefits primarily in:

- **Events lasting longer than 2.5–3 hours**
- **Athletes who have trained their gut** over weeks and months
- **Situations where intensity is high enough to rely heavily on exogenous carbohydrate**

For shorter efforts, recreational athletes, or those with sensitive stomachs, pushing carb intake too high can backfire spectacularly—leading to nausea, bloating, and the dreaded "GI distress" that ends races early.

---

## The Gut Is a Trainable Muscle

Perhaps the most important takeaway from Roche's success—and from the broader high-carb movement—is that **gut tolerance is trainable**.

Just as you wouldn't run a marathon without building mileage, you shouldn't show up on race day planning to consume 120 grams of carbs per hour if you've never practiced it. Research shows that the gut adapts to increased carbohydrate intake over time, upregulating carbohydrate transporters and improving gastric emptying.

### Practical Gut Training Protocol

| Week | Target Intake (per hour) | Focus |
|------|--------------------------|-------|
| 1–2 | 40–60 g | Establish baseline tolerance |
| 3–4 | 60–80 g | Introduce multiple transportable carbs |
| 5–6 | 80–100 g | Practice during long runs at race pace |
| 7–8 | 100–120 g | Simulate race-day fueling, including solid foods |

**Coaching tip:** Practice fueling during your longest, hardest workouts—not just easy runs. The gut responds to stress, and you want to know how your stomach behaves when you're breathing hard and running downhill.

## Beyond Carbs: The Recovery-Sleep Connection

Here's where the conversation gets interesting—and where the original headline's focus on carbs misses a crucial piece of the puzzle.

Roche has been candid that his fueling strategy works in tandem with meticulous attention to recovery. And the single most powerful recovery tool available to any athlete, at any level, is **sleep**.

### What the Research Shows

- **Sleep deprivation impairs glycogen synthesis.** Studies show that sleep restriction reduces muscle glycogen storage and impairs post-exercise recovery.
- **Sleep extension improves performance.** Research on Stanford athletes found that extending sleep to 10 hours per night improved sprint times, reaction time, and mood.
- **Sleep is when adaptation happens.** Growth hormone peaks during deep sleep, and this is when muscle repair and adaptation to training occur.

For ultrarunners logging 80–120 miles per week, sleep is not a luxury—it's a training session.

### Practical Sleep Strategies for Endurance Athletes

1. **Prioritize 8–10 hours of sleep opportunity** during heavy training blocks. Yes, that's a lot—but it's what the research supports for high-volume athletes.
2. **Protect the hour before bed.** Dim lights, avoid screens, and keep the room cool (65–68°F / 18–20°C).
3. **Consider a short nap (20–30 minutes)** after long morning sessions to accelerate recovery without disrupting nighttime sleep.
4. **Track sleep quality, not just quantity.** Wearables can be useful, but how you feel upon waking is a reliable gauge.
5. **Don't sacrifice sleep for early workouts.** If you're choosing between a 5 a.m. run and an extra hour of sleep, the sleep may win more often than you think.

## Fueling for Different Fitness Levels

High-carb fueling isn't a one-size-fits-all prescription. Here's how to approach it based on where you are:

### Beginners (First Ultra or Long-Distance Event)

- **Target 40–60 g carbs/hour** during training and racing.
- Focus on **consistency** rather than quantity—eating small amounts frequently is easier on the gut than large boluses.
- Practice with **real foods** as well as sports nutrition products.
- Don't compare yourself to elite athletes. Their protocols are built on years of gut training and high training volumes.

### Intermediate Athletes

- **Target 60–90 g carbs/hour**, using multiple transportable carbohydrate sources.
- Begin structured gut training 8–12 weeks before your goal race.
- Experiment with **different product types** (gels, chews, drinks, real food) to find what works for you.
- Monitor GI symptoms and adjust accordingly.

### Advanced/Elite Athletes

- **Target 90–120+ g carbs/hour**, but only after extensive gut training.
- Work with a sports dietitian to individualize your plan.
- Consider **periodized carbohydrate intake**—higher on race day and key workouts, lower on easy days.
- Pay equal attention to sleep, recovery, and stress management.

## Safety Considerations and Red Flags

Before you start downing gels like they're going out of style, consider these safety points:

- **GI distress is a warning sign.** Persistent nausea, bloating, or diarrhea during training means you're pushing too hard, too fast. Back off and rebuild.
- **Watch your hydration.** High carbohydrate intake requires adequate fluid to aid digestion. Aim for 16–24 oz of fluid per hour, adjusted for sweat rate and conditions.
- **Don't neglect electrolytes.** High-carb fueling can dilute sodium intake if you're not careful. Include electrolyte sources, especially in heat.
- **Individualize.** Some athletes thrive on high carbs; others perform better with moderate intake and more fat oxidation. There is no universal protocol.
- **Consult a professional** if you have a history of GI issues, diabetes, or other metabolic conditions.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Full Picture

Carbs and sleep are two pillars of recovery, but they don't stand alone. A complete recovery strategy includes:

### Nutrition Timing

- **Post-workout window:** Consume 1–1.2 g/kg of carbohydrate and 0.3 g/kg of protein within 30–60 minutes of hard or long sessions.
- **Throughout the day:** Spread protein intake across 3–5 meals (20–40 g each) to support muscle repair.

### Active Recovery

- Easy movement—walking, cycling, swimming—promotes blood flow and reduces soreness without adding training stress.
- **Foam rolling and mobility work** can help address tightness before it becomes injury.

### Load Management

- The 10% rule (increasing weekly mileage by no more than 10%) remains a useful guideline, though individual tolerance varies.
- **Listen to your body.** Fatigue, persistent soreness, and declining performance are signs you need more recovery, not more training.

### Strength Training

- Two short sessions per week of **targeted strength work** (hip stability, core, calf raises) can reduce injury risk significantly. This is especially important for ultrarunners, whose repetitive motion patterns create imbalances over time.

## Coaching Perspective: What Actually Moves the Needle

From a coaching standpoint, the athletes who succeed at ultrarunning—and in fitness generally—share common traits that have nothing to do with the latest fueling trend:

1. **Consistency over intensity.** Showing up day after day beats occasional heroic efforts.
2. **Patience with progress.** Real adaptation takes months and years, not weeks. Expect to see meaningful gains in 8–12 weeks of consistent training, with true physiological adaptation taking 6–12 months.
3. **Curiosity and self-awareness.** The best athletes experiment, track results, and adjust. They treat their body as a laboratory, not a machine.
4. **Respect for recovery.** They sleep, they eat, they take rest days without guilt.
5. **A long-term perspective.** Ultrarunning is a sport of decades, not seasons.

---

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

Not everyone has access to the same gear or terrain. Here's how to adapt:

- **No access to trails?** Use treadmill incline workouts, stair repeats, or hilly road routes to build climbing strength.
- **Limited time?** Focus on quality over quantity—tempo runs, hill repeats, and long runs on weekends can build substantial fitness on 5–6 hours per week.
- **No sports nutrition products?** Real foods work: bananas, dates, boiled potatoes with salt, rice cakes, and homemade energy bars are all effective and often easier on the stomach.
- **Traveling or in heat?** Adjust carbohydrate and fluid intake upward, and practice fueling in similar conditions before race day.

---

## The Bottom Line

David Roche's success is real, and high-carb fueling is a legitimate strategy backed by evolving science. But the headline—"Crazy High Carbs"—oversimplifies a complex system.

The secret to ultrarunning success isn't just carbs. It's the **integration** of smart fueling, gut training, quality sleep, consistent recovery, and patient, progressive training. Carbs are fuel, but sleep is repair. Training is the stimulus, but recovery is where adaptation happens.

Whether you're aiming for your first 50K or your tenth 100-miler, the principles are the same: start where you are, progress gradually, practice your race-day strategy in training, and prioritize the fundamentals—sleep, nutrition, and consistency—over any single "secret."

Your body is capable of remarkable things. Give it the fuel, the rest, and the time it needs—and then go find out what you can do.
