# Running Through the Fog: What a Former College Athlete's Depression Journey Teaches Us About Exercise and Mental Health

When a former college athlete describes spending years "trying to power through" depression—cycling through multiple oral antidepressants before finding one that worked—it resonates with anyone who has laced up running shoes hoping to outrun their own mind. His story isn't unusual. It's a window into one of the most important conversations in modern sports science: the complex, sometimes complicated relationship between intense training, athletic identity, and mental health.

## The Athlete's Paradox: When Movement Isn't Enough

Exercise is one of the most evidence-supported interventions for mild-to-moderate depression. A 2023 meta-analysis published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, encompassing over 1,000 trials and 128,000 participants, found that physical activity is highly beneficial for improving symptoms of depression and anxiety across clinical and non-clinical populations. Walking, jogging, yoga, and strength training all showed meaningful effects—with moderate-to-vigorous intensity appearing most effective.

Yet the former college athlete's story reveals a critical nuance: **movement helps, but it is not a cure-all, and it does not replace professional treatment.** He tried several medications over years before finding one that worked—a reminder that mental health care is often iterative, requiring patience and partnership with clinicians.

For runners especially, there's an added layer: the "runner's high" is real but temporary. Endorphins and endocannabinoids can lift mood for hours, not weeks. And for some athletes, compulsive training can become a form of avoidance—a way to numb rather than process.

## What the Science Says (and Doesn't Say)

Here's what we know with reasonable confidence:

- **Regular aerobic exercise** at moderate intensity (think: a conversational-pace run) can reduce depressive symptoms comparably to some first-line treatments for mild-to-moderate cases.
- **Outdoor running** ("green exercise") appears to offer additional mood benefits compared with indoor equivalents, according to a growing body of environmental psychology research.
- **Consistency beats intensity.** The mental health benefits accumulate with regular sessions—roughly 150 minutes per week of moderate activity is a solid baseline, per WHO and CDC guidelines.
- **Exercise is not a substitute for medication or therapy** in moderate-to-severe depression. The strongest outcomes come from combined approaches: movement, professional care, sleep, nutrition, and social connection.

## Practical Training Advice for Runners at Every Level

### Beginners (0–3 months)
- **Start with walk-run intervals:** 1 minute jogging, 2 minutes walking, repeated for 20–30 minutes, three times per week.
- **Form cues:** Land with your foot under your hips (not far out in front), keep your cadence light and quick, and relax your shoulders. Avoid overstriding—it's the most common beginner error and a frequent cause of shin and knee pain.
- **Realistic timeline:** Expect the first 2–3 weeks to feel hard. Aerobic adaptation takes 4–6 weeks; mood benefits often appear sooner—sometimes after a single session.
- **Safety:** If you have cardiovascular risk factors, check with a physician before starting. Never increase weekly mileage by more than 10%.

### Intermediate (3–12 months of consistent running)
- **Build a base:** 3–4 runs per week, with one longer effort. Keep 80% of running easy (able to hold a conversation).
- **Add strength work:** Two 20-minute sessions weekly targeting glutes, calves, and core reduce injury risk substantially. Single-leg bridges, calf raises, and planks are excellent starting points.
- **Watch for the "too much, too soon" trap:** Many runners use training as emotional regulation. If you notice irritability, sleep disruption, or a compulsive need to run despite pain or fatigue, that's a signal to pull back.

### Advanced (experienced racers)
- **Periodize deliberately.** Overtraining syndrome shares symptoms with depression—fatigue, low mood, poor sleep. Track resting heart rate and subjective energy to distinguish the two.
- **Protect recovery:** Sleep 7–9 hours, take at least one full rest day weekly, and schedule a down week every 3–4 weeks.
- **Consider professional support proactively:** Sports psychologists and therapists who understand athlete identity can be invaluable—especially when injury or performance dips threaten self-worth.

## Nutrition for Mood and Performance

What you eat influences how you feel—and runners have specific needs:

- **Carbohydrates are not the enemy.** Adequate carbs support serotonin production and fuel training. Aim for 3–5 g/kg body weight daily for moderate training.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** (fatty fish, walnuts, flaxseed) have modest but consistent evidence for supporting mood. Aim for two servings of fish weekly or consider a supplement after consulting a clinician.
- **Vitamin D and iron:** Deficiency in either is linked to fatigue and low mood. Runners—especially those training indoors or menstruating—should test periodically.
- **Hydration matters:** Even mild dehydration (1–2% body mass) can impair mood and concentration.
- **Caffeine timing:** A morning coffee before a run is fine for most, but late-day caffeine disrupts sleep—and sleep is foundational to mental health.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

- **Sleep is training.** Prioritize 7–9 hours. Poor sleep both worsens depression and increases injury risk.
- **Respect pain.** Sharp, localized pain that worsens with running is a stop sign, not a challenge. Cross-train (cycling, swimming, elliptical) while you recover.
- **Alternatives for different environments:** No trails? Treadmill running with a slight incline mimics outdoor effort. No gym? Bodyweight strength circuits—squats, lunges, push-ups, planks—require zero equipment. Bad weather? Indoor cycling or a brisk walk still counts.
- **Build a support network.** Running clubs and training partners provide accountability and social connection—both powerful protective factors for mental health.

## Coaching Perspective: What Experienced Coaches Notice

Veteran coaches often spot the warning signs before athletes do: a runner who never takes rest days, who panics at the thought of missing a workout, or whose mood collapses after a bad race. Good coaching means asking "How are you *feeling*?" as often as "How was your split?" If training has become your only coping tool, diversify your support system—therapy, community, sleep, and nutrition all matter.

## The Bottom Line

The former college athlete's story is ultimately one of persistence—and of recognizing that strength isn't the same as suffering silently. Running can be a powerful ally in mental health, but it works best alongside professional care, honest self-awareness, and sustainable habits. Whether you're running your first mile or your hundredth race, the goal isn't to outrun your mind. It's to move forward *with* it—well-supported, well-fed, well-rested, and honest about when to ask for help.

*If you or someone you know is struggling with depression, reach out to a healthcare provider or a mental health crisis line. Exercise is a supplement to treatment—not a substitute for it.*
