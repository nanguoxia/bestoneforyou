# Steph Curry Wants You to Get Your Eyes Checked: What Every Athlete Should Know About Vision and Performance

When we think about the pillars of athletic performance, we usually rattle off the familiar list: strength, conditioning, mobility, nutrition, sleep, and recovery. Rarely does vision make the cut. Yet for Golden State Warriors superstar Steph Curry, one of the most decorated shooters in NBA history, an eye condition called **keratoconus** has been part of his story for years—and it's a reminder that sharp eyesight may be one of the most underrated performance assets in any athlete's toolkit.

Curry has used his platform to raise awareness about keratoconus, a progressive eye condition that affects the shape of the cornea. His message is simple but powerful: get your eyes checked. For fitness enthusiasts of every level, that advice carries real weight—not just for health, but for training quality, safety, and long-term performance.

## What Is Keratoconus?

Keratoconus is a condition in which the normally round, dome-shaped cornea (the clear front surface of the eye) gradually thins and bulges into a cone-like shape. This irregular shape distorts how light enters the eye, causing blurred or distorted vision, increased sensitivity to light, and difficulty seeing at night.

According to the American Academy of Ophthalmology and the National Eye Institute, keratoconus typically begins in the teenage years or early twenties and affects roughly **1 in 2,000 people**, though some estimates suggest the true prevalence may be higher due to underdiagnosis. It often progresses slowly over a decade or more and can affect both eyes, though usually asymmetrically.

Risk factors include:

- **Genetics** — a family history of keratoconus increases risk
- **Frequent eye rubbing** — a well-documented contributing factor
- **Certain conditions** — asthma, allergies, and Down syndrome are associated with higher rates
- **Age** — onset is most common in adolescence and early adulthood

Treatment ranges from glasses and specialty contact lenses (like rigid gas-permeable or scleral lenses) to a procedure called **corneal collagen cross-linking**, which can slow or halt progression. In advanced cases, corneal transplantation may be necessary.

## Why Vision Matters More Than You Think for Fitness

Here's where this becomes relevant for every lifter, runner, cyclist, and weekend warrior. Vision isn't just about seeing the world clearly—it's a **neuromuscular input system** that directly affects how you move, balance, and perform.

Research in sports vision science has shown that visual skills—dynamic visual acuity, depth perception, peripheral awareness, and eye-hand coordination—contribute meaningfully to athletic performance. A frequently cited review in *Optometry and Vision Science* highlighted that elite athletes often outperform non-athletes on measures of visual processing speed and accuracy, though the causal direction (training vs. selection) remains debated.

Practical implications for your training:

- **Balance and proprioception:** Your visual system works with your inner ear and joint receptors to maintain balance. Blurry or distorted vision can impair stability, especially during single-leg work, squats, and lunges.
- **Depth perception:** Crucial for catching, throwing, jumping, and landing mechanics. Poor depth perception increases injury risk during plyometrics and agility drills.
- **Reaction time:** Visual processing speed influences how quickly you respond to a shifting barbell, an uneven trail, or a defender.
- **Form and safety:** If you can't clearly see your setup—whether on a bench press or a running trail—your risk of technical breakdown and injury rises.

## Practical Steps: Get Your Eyes Checked and Optimize Your Vision

Whether you're a beginner or a seasoned athlete, here's how to turn this information into action.

### 1. Schedule a Comprehensive Eye Exam

A basic vision screening at a doctor's office or DMV isn't enough. Ask for a **comprehensive dilated eye exam** from an optometrist or ophthalmologist. This can detect keratoconus, glaucoma, retinal issues, and other conditions in their early stages—when intervention is most effective.

**How often?**
- Every **2–4 years** for adults aged 18–39 with no risk factors
- Every **1–2 years** for adults 40+ or those with risk factors (diabetes, family history of eye disease, high myopia)
- **Annually** if you wear contacts, have keratoconus, or notice changes in vision

### 2. Don't Ignore Subtle Changes

Blurred vision, frequent headaches after training, eye strain, halos around lights at night, or needing to squint more often are all signals to book an appointment. Athletes often dismiss these as fatigue—don't.

### 3. Stop Rubbing Your Eyes

This is one of the most actionable pieces of advice for keratoconus prevention. Chronic eye rubbing is strongly associated with the condition's development and progression. If allergies or dryness make you rub, treat the underlying cause with lubricating drops, antihistamines, or a humidifier.

### 4. Optimize Nutrition for Eye Health

While diet can't cure keratoconus, certain nutrients support overall eye health and may slow age-related decline:

- **Lutein and zeaxanthin** — found in leafy greens (kale, spinach), eggs, and corn; these carotenoids concentrate in the macula and help filter blue light.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** — from fatty fish (salmon, sardines) or algae oil; support tear production and may reduce dry eye symptoms.
- **Vitamin C and E** — antioxidants found in citrus, nuts, and seeds; part of the AREDS2 formula studied for macular health.
- **Zinc** — supports retinal health; found in oysters, beef, and legumes.
- **Hydration** — adequate fluid intake supports tear film and reduces dry eye, which can worsen visual clarity during training.

A practical plate: a spinach and salmon salad with walnuts, citrus, and olive oil hits several of these targets in one meal.

### 5. Consider Sports Vision Training

For athletes looking to sharpen visual skills, sports vision training programs—using tools like stroboscopic glasses, reaction balls, and peripheral awareness drills—have grown in popularity. Evidence is mixed but promising, particularly for reaction time and hand-eye coordination. If you're curious, look for a certified sports vision optometrist rather than a generic online program.

## Training and Recovery Considerations for Athletes With Vision Conditions

If you have keratoconus or another vision condition, you can absolutely train hard—but a few adjustments help:

- **Use appropriate correction:** Scleral lenses are often the go-to for keratoconus athletes because they provide stable, clear vision and protect the cornea. Discuss options with your eye care provider.
- **Protect your eyes:** Wear polycarbonate sports goggles or sunglasses with UV protection during outdoor training, cycling, or contact sports.
- **Prioritize balance work:** Incorporate single-leg stands, Bosu ball exercises, and proprioceptive drills to compensate for any visual input deficits.
- **Train in good lighting:** Poor lighting magnifies visual challenges. If you run outdoors, choose well-lit paths or a treadmill when needed.
- **Listen to fatigue:** Visual fatigue can compound physical fatigue. If your eyes feel strained, take a break, hydrate, and rest.

## Timelines: What Realistic Progress Looks Like

- **Vision correction:** Most people notice significantly clearer vision within days to weeks of getting proper lenses or treatment.
- **Balance and proprioception improvements:** Expect measurable gains in **4–8 weeks** of consistent balance training.
- **Sports vision training:** Studies suggest improvements in reaction time and visual acuity tasks within **6–12 weeks**, though transfer to sport-specific performance varies.
- **Keratoconus progression:** Cross-linking can stabilize the cornea in most patients, but results are typically evaluated over **6–12 months**. Early detection remains the single most important factor.

## The Bottom Line

Steph Curry's willingness to talk about keratoconus is a gift to anyone who trains. It reminds us that performance isn't just about muscle and lungs—it's about the sensory systems that feed them information. Getting your eyes checked isn't a one-time chore; it's a foundational habit for anyone who wants to move well, train safely, and perform at their best.

So take a page from Curry's playbook: book that eye exam, protect your vision, fuel your eyes with the right nutrients, and don't ignore the subtle signs. Your body will thank you—and so will your training log.
