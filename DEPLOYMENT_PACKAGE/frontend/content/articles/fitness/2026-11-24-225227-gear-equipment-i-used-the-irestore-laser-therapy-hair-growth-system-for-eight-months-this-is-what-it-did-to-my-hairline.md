# I Used a Laser Therapy Hair Growth System for Eight Months—Here's What Actually Happened to My Hairline (and What It Means for Your Wellness Routine)

**A science-based look at low-level laser therapy, plus the training, nutrition, and recovery habits that actually protect your hair as you age**

---

## The Honest Starting Point

Let me be clear about my goal from day one: I wasn't trying to regrow a full head of hair. I was trying not to lose any more of it.

That distinction matters, because it's the same one that separates realistic fitness goals from fantasy ones. You don't walk into the gym expecting to add 50 pounds to your bench press in a month—you train for maintenance, then incremental gain. Hair is no different. The evidence around most hair-loss interventions points toward *slowing progression* and *preserving what you have* far more reliably than reversing years of loss.

Over eight months, I used an FDA-cleared low-level laser therapy (LLLT) device—the iRestore system—while continuing to train, eat, and recover like someone who cares about long-term health. Here's what I learned, what the science actually supports, and how hair health fits into the broader wellness picture that strength athletes and runners tend to overlook.

---

## What Low-Level Laser Therapy Actually Is

LLLT—sometimes called photobiomodulation—uses red light (typically around 650 nm) to stimulate cells in the hair follicle. The proposed mechanism: light energy is absorbed by mitochondria, boosting cellular energy (ATP) production and improving blood flow to the follicle, which may extend the growth phase (anagen) of the hair cycle.

A few evidence-based anchor points:

- **FDA clearance** for androgenetic alopecia (pattern hair loss) exists for several devices, including the iRestore line. Clearance means the device is *safe* for its intended use—not that it's proven to regrow a full head of hair.
- **Clinical research** on LLLT is promising but modest. Randomized trials and meta-analyses generally report measurable increases in hair density and hair count versus sham devices, with effects that are real but incremental—often in the range of a few to roughly a dozen additional hairs per square centimeter over several months in responders.
- **Response varies widely.** Some users see visible improvement; others see only maintenance; a minority see little change. This is the single most important thing to understand before you spend money.

**Realistic timeline:** Most studies and manufacturer guidance suggest 16–24 weeks before meaningful change, with continued use required to maintain results. If you stop, benefits typically fade. Think of it as a maintenance protocol, not a cure.

## My Eight-Month Experience (Without the Hype)

**Months 1–2:** Nothing visible. This is normal and where most people quit. Consistency is the entire game—treat it like a training program where the first block is adaptation, not results.

**Months 3–4:** I noticed less shedding in the shower and on my pillow. This is the most commonly reported early benefit and aligns with reduced inflammation and improved follicle health. It's subtle but meaningful.

**Months 5–8:** My hairline looked *stable*—not dramatically thicker, but not receding further. Density at the temples felt marginally improved. Friends didn't notice; I did. That's an honest result.

**The verdict:** For me, it functioned as a maintenance tool. If your goal is "don't lose more," that's a legitimate win. If your goal is "regrow my teenage hairline," temper expectations and talk to a dermatologist about combination approaches.

## Safety and Proper Use (Read This Before Buying Anything)

- **Follow the device's session time exactly.** Overuse doesn't accelerate results; it risks irritation. Most protocols are short (roughly 10–25 minutes, several times per week depending on the model).
- **Keep the device moving** per instructions—don't park it on one spot.
- **Skip it if you have active scalp inflammation, lesions, or are on photosensitizing medications** without checking with a physician.
- **Not for everyone:** LLLT isn't a substitute for medical evaluation. Sudden, patchy, or rapid hair loss warrants a dermatologist visit—thyroid issues, iron deficiency, and other conditions mimic pattern loss.
- **Protect your eyes** if the device emits light near the face.

## The Part Fitness Enthusiasts Ignore: Hair Is a Wellness Signal

Here's where this becomes a genuine fitness article. Hair loss and hair quality are downstream of the same systems you train and feed every day. Ignoring them while chasing PRs is a blind spot.

### 1. Overtraining and Recovery

Chronic high training loads without adequate recovery elevate cortisol and can push more follicles into the resting (telogen) phase—temporary but noticeable shedding. **Actionable cue:** If you're in a heavy block and notice increased shedding, audit sleep (aim 7–9 hours) and deload every 4–6 weeks. Recovery isn't optional; it's where adaptation—and healthy hair—happens.

### 2. Nutrition for Follicles

- **Protein:** Hair is largely keratin. Beginners and aggressive dieters often under-eat protein. Target roughly **1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight** for training individuals.
- **Iron and ferritin:** Low ferritin is a common, underdiagnosed driver of shedding, especially in endurance athletes and menstruating women. Get ferritin tested if shedding is persistent.
- **Vitamin D and zinc:** Both support normal hair cycling. Test before supplementing—more isn't better.
- **Avoid crash dieting.** Severe caloric restriction is a well-documented trigger for telogen effluvium.

### 3. Strength Training and Blood Flow

Resistance training improves circulation and metabolic health—both friendly to follicle function. There's no evidence that lifting *causes* hair loss; the old "testosterone from lifting makes you bald" claim is oversimplified. Genetic sensitivity to DHT drives pattern loss, not your squat session.

### 4. Running and Endurance Considerations

High-volume endurance training raises oxidative stress and can increase shedding if recovery and fueling lag. **Practical tip:** Prioritize carbohydrate availability around long runs and keep ferritin in range—under-fueling hurts performance *and* hair.

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

- **No laser device?** Focus first on the free levers: sleep, protein, ferritin/vitamin D status, stress management, and avoiding harsh styling and heat.
- **Topical alternatives** (e.g., minoxidil) are evidence-backed for pattern loss—discuss with a clinician, as combining approaches is common.
- **Gym-agnostic recovery:** Sauna, mobility work, and consistent sleep hygiene support the same stress systems that affect hair.
- **Travel:** Keep session consistency with a compact device or schedule around travel rather than abandoning the protocol.

---

## Coaching-Perspective Takeaways

1. **Set a maintenance goal, not a miracle goal.** Measure baseline photos monthly under the same lighting. Progress is slow and subtle.
2. **Commit to 6 months minimum** before judging any intervention. Quitting at week 6 guarantees failure.
3. **Treat hair like recovery:** it responds to consistency, not intensity.
4. **Get labs if shedding persists**—ferritin, vitamin D, thyroid. Rule out causes before buying gadgets.
5. **Stack interventions.** LLLT plus good nutrition, sleep, and medical guidance beats any single tool.

---

## Bottom Line

Eight months of laser therapy gave me what I asked for: a stable hairline and less shedding—not a dramatic regrowth story. That's an honest, evidence-consistent outcome. The bigger lesson is that hair health sits on the same foundation as athletic health: consistent training, adequate protein and micronutrients, real recovery, and medical check-ins when something changes.

If you're pursuing wellness goals, don't silo them. Your hairline, your lifts, and your long-run pace are all reading from the same physiological script. Train smart, eat enough, recover deliberately—and set expectations that science can actually deliver on.
