# Pickleball Is the Fastest-Growing Sport in America—and It's a Surprisingly Serious Workout

**The racquet sport everyone's talking about may be the cardio you've been avoiding. Here's what the science says, how to train for it, and how to avoid the injuries that send players to the sidelines.**

---

## A Hobby That Hits Back

I picked up pickleball the way most people do: a friend insisted, I showed up skeptical, and forty minutes later I was drenched in sweat, gasping between points, and quietly hooked. What began as a casual Saturday morning has become a standing appointment I both dread and crave—a hobby that is, without exaggeration, kicking my ass.

I'm not alone. According to the Sports & Fitness Industry Association (SFIA), pickleball has been the fastest-growing sport in the United States for several consecutive years, with participation surging past 13 million players—a nearly fourfold increase in under a decade. The appeal is obvious: low barrier to entry, short learning curve, and a social atmosphere that feels more like a backyard barbecue than a gym session.

But here's what the highlight reels don't show you: pickleball is a legitimate workout. And if you treat it like a leisurely hobby rather than a sport, it will humble you—or worse, injure you.

---

## The Physiology of the Paddle

Pickleball doesn't look hard. The court is small (44 feet long, roughly a third of a tennis court), the ball is light, and the underhand serve seems forgiving. What the eye misses, the heart rate monitor catches.

Research published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* and related journals has found that recreational pickleball play can elicit heart rates in the range of 60–70% of maximum—solidly in the moderate-to-vigorous intensity zone that guidelines from the World Health Organization and the American College of Sports Medicine recommend for cardiovascular health. In doubles play, players often sustain this for 60–90 minutes with only brief pauses.

The demands are uniquely hybrid. Pickleball is a stop-and-start sport: short explosive bursts of lateral movement, quick direction changes, and reactive lunges, punctuated by brief recovery windows. That profile builds:

- **Aerobic capacity** through sustained rally play
- **Anaerobic power** through repeated short sprints and quick transitions
- **Agility and reaction time**, particularly at the non-volley zone (the "kitchen")
- **Rotational core strength** from groundstrokes and volleys
- **Shoulder and forearm endurance** from sustained paddle work

A 2023 review in *Journal of Aging and Physical Activity* also linked regular pickleball participation to improved balance and functional fitness in older adults—one reason the sport's demographics skew older even as younger players flood in.

The catch? That same stop-and-start profile is precisely what makes the sport demanding on joints and connective tissue—especially for players who show up cold, play hard, and skip the warm-up.

## Why It's Kicking Your Ass (and What to Do About It)

If your first few sessions left you sore in places you forgot you had, you're in good company. Here's the breakdown of why, and how to train around it.

### 1. You're doing more lateral work than you're used to

Most gym routines are sagittal—running, cycling, squats, pressing. Pickleball is aggressively frontal-plane and rotational. Your hips, adductors, and ankles are working in ranges your usual training never touches.

**Fix:** Add lateral movement to your week. Two sets of 10 lateral band walks, 3×8 side lunges per leg, and 3×10 minutes of light agility ladder work will pay dividends within 4–6 weeks. Beginners should start with bodyweight only; advanced players can add a resistance band above the knees or load side lunges with dumbbells.

### 2. The start-stop rhythm spikes your heart rate

New players often sprint to every ball, then stand still between points—a pattern that spikes heart rate and taxes the cardiovascular system unevenly.

**Fix:** Build a base with 2–3 weekly zone-2 sessions (30–45 minutes of conversational-pace cardio—brisk walking, easy cycling, or rowing). Intermediates can add one weekly interval session: 8×30 seconds hard / 90 seconds easy. This mirrors the rally-recovery rhythm of the sport.

### 3. Your grip and shoulder are working overtime

Tight gripping and repetitive volleys are a recipe for elbow and shoulder irritation—the pickleball version of tennis elbow.

**Fix:** Loosen your grip between points (think "hold a small bird"). Add 2×15 external rotations with a light band, 2×12 face pulls, and 2×15 wrist extensor curls with a light dumbbell, twice weekly. If you have no equipment, isometric wrist holds against a wall for 3×20 seconds work well.

### 4. Your ankles and calves take the brunt of quick direction changes

Lateral lunges and sudden stops load the ankles heavily. Ankle sprains are among the most common pickleball injuries reported in sports medicine literature.

**Fix:** Prioritize calf raises (3×12–15, progressing to single-leg), single-leg balance holds (3×20–30 seconds per side), and ankle circles before play. Advanced players can add single-leg hops and lateral bounds—but only after mastering controlled landings.

## A Practical Training Week

Here's a template that scales from beginner to advanced. Adjust volume by fitness level and recovery capacity.

**Beginner (0–2 sessions/week of play):**
- 2× full-body strength (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry)—30 minutes
- 2× zone-2 cardio—30 minutes
- 1× mobility session (hips, ankles, thoracic spine)—15 minutes

**Intermediate (2–3 play sessions/week):**
- 2× strength with added lateral and rotational work (side lunges, Pallof presses, rotational rows)
- 1× interval session
- 1–2× zone-2 cardio
- Daily 5-minute ankle and shoulder prep before play

**Advanced (3–4 play sessions/week):**
- 2× strength (one heavier, one power-focused: med ball throws, lateral bounds)
- 1× sprint/agility session
- 1× zone-2 recovery cardio
- Structured deload every 4–6 weeks

**Coaching tip:** Treat play sessions as training load. If you play three hard sessions in a week, don't stack heavy leg days on top. Spread strength work at least 24 hours from intense play when possible.

## Fuel and Hydration: The Overlooked Edge

Pickleball is often played in heat, in short bursts, and for longer than players plan. A few practical guidelines:

- **Before play:** A mixed meal 2–3 hours out (carbohydrate + moderate protein, low fat). If pressed for time, a banana and a handful of nuts 45–60 minutes before works fine.
- **During play:** Water is sufficient for sessions under 60 minutes. For longer or hot-weather sessions, add electrolytes—sodium matters more than most recreational players realize.
- **After play:** 20–30 grams of protein within a couple of hours supports recovery, especially for players over 40, where research suggests higher per-meal protein intake helps preserve muscle.
- **Alcohol caveat:** The post-game beer is a ritual, not a recovery strategy. It impairs rehydration and muscle protein synthesis. Enjoy it—just don't count it as nutrition.

---

## Realistic Timelines

- **Weeks 1–2:** Soreness in new places. Expect calves, hips, and forearms to complain. Keep play to 2 sessions and prioritize sleep.
- **Weeks 3–6:** Coordination improves noticeably; rally length increases; soreness fades as your lateral tissue adapts.
- **Weeks 6–12:** Cardiovascular fitness catches up—you'll recover between points faster. Strength work starts showing in your movement quality.
- **Months 3–6:** Skill and fitness compound. This is when players typically notice they can play back-to-back days without feeling wrecked—provided they've been consistent with prep and recovery.

No timeline is guaranteed; consistency beats intensity, and individual recovery varies with age, sleep, and training history.

## Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

1. **Warm up for 8–10 minutes.** Dynamic movement—leg swings, walking lunges, banded lateral steps, light paddle rallying—raises tissue temperature and primes the nervous system. Static stretching before play does little for injury prevention.
2. **Learn the split step and stay low.** Bending at the hips and knees rather than the waist protects your lower back and puts you in position to move in any direction.
3. **Respect the kitchen line.** Lunging forward for a ball near the non-volley zone is a classic mechanism for falls and ankle injuries. Move your feet; don't dive.
4. **Progress play volume gradually.** The "too much, too soon" rule applies: increase weekly play by no more than about 10–20%, especially in your first two months.
5. **Address niggles early.** Persistent elbow, shoulder, or ankle pain that lasts more than a week warrants a physio or sports medicine consult—not "playing through it."

---

## No Court? No Problem

Not everyone has access to a court or a partner. The fitness adaptations transfer:

- **Solo:** Wall rallies with a paddle and ball build hand-eye coordination; shadow drills (split step, lateral shuffle, cross-step) build movement patterns; a rebounder or sturdy wall substitutes for a partner.
- **No paddle:** Tennis or badminton work fine for movement prep; a resistance band and a wall cover most strength needs.
- **Home training:** Bodyweight lateral lunges, single-leg balance, banded rotations, and skipping rope replicate much of the sport's physical demand.

---

## The Bottom Line

Pickleball earns its reputation as fun—but it deserves respect as training. The players who stay healthy and improve fastest aren't the ones who play the most; they're the ones who prepare, recover, and progress sensibly. Treat it like a sport, fuel it like a sport, and it will reward you with genuinely impressive cardiovascular fitness, agility, and—if my experience is any guide—a hobby that keeps you honest.
