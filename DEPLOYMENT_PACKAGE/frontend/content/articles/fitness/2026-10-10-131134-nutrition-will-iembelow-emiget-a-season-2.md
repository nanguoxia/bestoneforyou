# The "Below" Effect: What a Deep-Sea Thriller Can Teach Us About Nutrition and Recovery

If you've spent any time on Netflix lately, you've probably noticed *Below*—the claustrophobic sea-monster thriller starring Josh Hartnett. And if you're like a lot of viewers, you finished it and immediately asked the question that's now dominating fitness forums and streaming subreddits alike: *Will there be a Season 2?*

Here's the short answer: Hartnett himself has described the project as a limited series—for now. That "for now" is doing a lot of work. But rather than speculate endlessly about renewal odds, let's use the show's central tension as a teaching tool. Because *Below* is, at its core, a story about what happens when you're trapped in a hostile environment with limited resources, mounting stress, and no clear exit.

That's not so different from a grueling training block, a weight-cut, or the final weeks of marathon prep. And the nutrition and recovery lessons are surprisingly transferable.

---

## The Pressure Cooker Problem: Stress, Cortisol, and Why "Pushing Through" Backfires

In *Below*, the characters are under constant, unrelenting pressure. The hull creaks. Something is out there. Every decision carries life-or-death stakes.

Your body doesn't know the difference between a sea monster and a deadline. Chronic stress—whether from training load, caloric restriction, or life—elevates cortisol, and cortisol is catabolic. It breaks down muscle, disrupts sleep, and encourages fat storage, particularly around the midsection.

**Recent exercise science finding:** A 2023 review in *Sports Medicine* found that athletes in a caloric deficit experienced significantly greater cortisol responses to training compared to those eating at maintenance. In plain terms: if you're dieting hard *and* training hard, your stress hormones are working against your goals.

### Actionable Advice for Different Levels

**Beginners:** Don't stack stressors. If you're new to lifting, don't simultaneously start a severe diet. Pick one goal for 8–12 weeks—either build the habit of training or clean up your nutrition. Trying to do both aggressively is the fastest route to burnout and injury.

**Intermediate:** Aim for a moderate deficit of 300–500 calories per day if fat loss is the goal. Any deeper, and you're inviting cortisol spikes, poor sleep, and stalled progress.

**Advanced:** If you're in a prolonged deficit (12+ weeks), consider a planned "diet break" of 1–2 weeks at maintenance calories. Research from the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* suggests this can restore leptin and thyroid hormones, improving subsequent fat loss.

**Safety cue:** Watch for signs of overreaching—persistent fatigue, elevated resting heart rate, irritability, and disrupted sleep. These are your body's version of the hull creaking. Listen.

## Fueling Under Pressure: What to Eat When You're Stretched Thin

The characters in *Below* are rationing supplies. You shouldn't be. Under-fueling is one of the most common mistakes I see in athletes and fitness enthusiasts alike.

**Data point:** A 2024 study in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* found that female athletes in a low-energy availability state had a 2–4x higher risk of stress fractures. Male athletes aren't immune either—relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S) affects both sexes and impairs everything from bone density to immune function.

### Practical Nutrition Framework

**Protein:** Target 1.6–2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. Spread it across 3–5 meals, with 0.3–0.4g/kg per meal to maximize muscle protein synthesis.

**Carbohydrates:** Don't fear them. If you're training more than 3–4 hours per week, carbs are your primary fuel. Aim for 3–5g/kg on training days, more if you're doing endurance work.

**Fats:** 0.8–1.0g/kg for hormonal health. Don't drop below 0.5g/kg for extended periods.

**Hydration:** Even mild dehydration (2% of body weight) impairs strength and endurance. A simple rule: drink 500ml (16–20oz) of water 2 hours before training, and sip 150–250ml every 15–20 minutes during.

### Alternatives for Different Environments

- **No kitchen access?** Rotisserie chicken, pre-cooked rice pouches, and frozen vegetables are your friends.
- **Plant-based?** Combine legumes and grains for complete protein, and consider a pea/rice protein blend.
- **Budget constraints?** Eggs, canned tuna, oats, and frozen spinach deliver enormous nutritional value per dollar.

## Recovery: The Life Raft You Keep Ignoring

In *Below*, the characters survive because they find moments of rest between crises. You need the same.

**Coaching insight:** The athletes who improve fastest aren't the ones who train hardest—they're the ones who recover best. I've seen intermediate lifters outpace advanced ones simply because they slept 8 hours and took deload weeks seriously.

### Recovery Checklist

1. **Sleep:** 7–9 hours. This is non-negotiable. Sleep deprivation reduces testosterone, impairs glucose metabolism, and increases injury risk.
2. **Deload every 4–8 weeks:** Reduce volume by 40–50% for one week. You'll come back stronger.
3. **Active recovery:** Walking, swimming, or light cycling on rest days promotes blood flow without adding stress.
4. **Mobility work:** 10 minutes daily of targeted mobility (hips, shoulders, ankles) prevents the compensatory patterns that lead to injury.

**Injury prevention cue:** If a movement causes sharp pain, stop. Dull, diffuse soreness is normal. Sharp, localized pain is not. Consult a professional.

## Realistic Timelines: What to Actually Expect

- **Strength gains (beginner):** 4–8 weeks to see noticeable improvements.
- **Visible muscle definition:** 8–12 weeks with consistent training and nutrition.
- **Fat loss:** 0.5–1% of body weight per week is sustainable. Faster isn't better.
- **Endurance improvements:** 6–10 weeks for measurable VO2 max gains.

Anyone promising faster results is selling something.

---

## The Verdict on Season 2—and Your Own Progress

Will *Below* get a second season? Hartnett says it's a limited series. But limited doesn't mean finished. The same is true for your fitness journey. A "limited" training block—8, 12, 16 weeks—can produce remarkable change if you fuel properly, manage stress, and recover intentionally.

You don't need a sea monster to create urgency. You need a plan, patience, and the discipline to rest when your body signals it's time.

Stay fueled. Stay recovered. Stay curious.
