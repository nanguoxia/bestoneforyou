# Smooth Miles, Smarter Training: What the Cloudsurfer 3 Trend Says About Running Comfort and Performance

**Category:** Running

Comfort is having a moment in running—and for good reason. The latest iteration of On's everyday trainer, the Cloudsurfer 3, is built to make daily miles feel smooth and effortless, even as the volume adds up. But before you lace up a softer shoe and expect instant gains, it's worth understanding what "comfort" really means for performance, recovery, and injury risk—and how to build a training plan that makes the most of it.

## The Science Behind "Feeling Soft"

Softer, more cushioned shoes have become a dominant trend in running footwear, and the research helps explain why. A 2020 study in *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* found that greater shoe cushioning reduced impact loading rates during running—one of the mechanical factors linked to overuse injuries. Meanwhile, a 2022 meta-analysis in *Sports Medicine* suggested that while highly cushioned shoes can improve perceived comfort and reduce muscle soreness after long runs, they aren't automatically faster; in fact, stiffer, more responsive shoes tend to offer better running economy for some athletes.

The takeaway: comfort has real value—especially for high-mileage runners and beginners—but it's one tool, not a magic bullet. The best shoe is the one that matches your training goals, biomechanics, and current fitness level.

## Matching the Shoe to the Run

Here's how to think about cushioned daily trainers across fitness levels:

**Beginners (0–15 miles/week):** A comfortable, well-cushioned daily trainer is an excellent first shoe. Prioritize fit and stability over hype. Get fitted at a specialty running store if possible, and replace shoes every 300–500 miles—cushioning degrades long before the outsole wears through.

**Intermediate runners (15–35 miles/week):** Rotate two pairs—a cushioned daily trainer for easy and long runs, and a lighter, firmer shoe for tempo work or strides. A 2023 study in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports* found that rotating shoes was associated with a lower risk of running-related injury, possibly because it varies loading patterns.

**Advanced runners (35+ miles/week):** Keep the cushioned trainer in the easy-day rotation, but don't do all your quality work in maximal cushioning. Save the softest shoe for recovery runs and long efforts where comfort supports form late in the run.

## Practical Training Tips From the Coaching Side

- **The 80/20 rule still rules.** Roughly 80% of weekly mileage should be easy—conversational pace. A smooth, comfortable shoe pairs perfectly with this, but the discipline is in the pace, not the shoe.
- **Build mileage gradually.** Increase weekly volume by no more than 10% and take a down week every fourth week. A 2021 review in *Sports Medicine* reinforced that rapid training load spikes are among the strongest predictors of injury.
- **Add strides twice a week.** Finish two easy runs with 4–6 × 20-second relaxed accelerations. This maintains neuromuscular sharpness even when most miles are comfortable.
- **Practice form cues on easy days.** Run tall, land with a quiet footfall, and keep cadence relaxed—around 165–180 steps per minute for many runners, though individual variation is normal.

## Nutrition to Support Smooth Miles

- **Fuel your easy runs.** Runs under 60 minutes generally don't require fuel beforehand, but a small carbohydrate snack (a banana or toast) helps if you're running first thing.
- **Protein timing matters for recovery.** Aim for 20–30 grams of protein within a couple of hours of your run. A 2019 position stand from the International Society of Sports Nutrition supports spreading protein across the day (roughly 1.4–2.0 g/kg body weight for active adults).
- **Hydrate by thirst on short runs, plan on long ones.** For runs over 75–90 minutes, practice taking 30–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour and 400–800 mg of sodium per liter of fluid—and rehearse this in training, not on race day.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

- **Sleep is the cheapest performance enhancer.** Adults need 7–9 hours; athletes in hard training blocks often benefit from the upper end.
- **Strength train twice a week.** Focus on single-leg work (split squats, step-ups), calf raises, and hip stability. A 2022 systematic review in *Sports Medicine* found strength training reduced running injury rates by roughly half in recreational runners.
- **Watch for warning signs.** Persistent shin, heel, or knee pain that worsens with running—rather than easing after a warm-up—warrants a rest day and, if it continues, a professional assessment.

## Alternatives for Every Environment

No treadmill or specialty store nearby? You can still train smart. Soft-surface trails and grass reduce impact; cycling and pool running maintain aerobic fitness during recovery weeks; and a firm, neutral everyday shoe from any reputable brand can fill the daily-trainer role while you decide on your next purchase. If you're unsure, a gait analysis—in person or via video—can guide the choice.

## The Bottom Line

A smoother, more comfortable ride can genuinely make daily running more enjoyable and support higher, healthier mileage—especially for beginners and high-volume runners. But comfort is a complement to smart training, not a substitute for it. Build your base gradually, keep most runs easy, strength train consistently, fuel and recover deliberately, and let a comfortable shoe do what it does best: help you keep showing up, mile after comfortable mile.

*Always consult a physician before starting a new training program, especially if you have existing injuries or health conditions.*
