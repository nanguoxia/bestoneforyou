# How to Build Your Legs With the Glute-Ham Raise

The glute-ham raise (GHR) is one of the most effective—and most overlooked—exercises for building powerful, resilient legs. While squats and deadlifts get most of the attention, the GHR targets the hamstrings and glutes through a unique combination of knee flexion and hip extension that few other movements can replicate. In fact, research using electromyography (EMG) has consistently shown the GHR produces some of the highest hamstring activation of any lower-body exercise—often rivaling or exceeding the Nordic hamstring curl, depending on technique and equipment.

If your leg training has plateaued, or if you're looking for a way to bulletproof your hamstrings against injury, this underrated exercise could be the MVP of your lower body program. Here's how to do it right, from your first rep to advanced progressions.

## Why the Glute-Ham Raise Deserves a Spot in Your Program

The GHR trains two distinct functions of the posterior chain simultaneously:

- **Knee flexion** — driven by the hamstrings, similar to a leg curl
- **Hip extension** — driven by the glutes and hamstrings working together

This dual action is why the GHR is so valuable. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in *Sports Medicine* (2019) found that exercises emphasizing eccentric hamstring loading—like the GHR and Nordic curl—are among the most effective for reducing hamstring strain injury risk. And since hamstring strains are among the most common injuries in sprinting, field sports, and even recreational running, that's a meaningful return on investment.

Beyond injury prevention, the GHR builds serious muscle. The strong eccentric (lowering) component creates high mechanical tension, a key driver of hypertrophy. It also develops the hamstrings in a lengthened position, which emerging research suggests may offer advantages for muscle growth compared to short-range movements.

## Setting Up: Equipment and Alternatives

The classic GHR requires a dedicated glute-ham developer bench, which you'll find in most commercial gyms. If you don't have access to one, don't skip this exercise—there are effective alternatives:

- **Nordic hamstring curl** — anchor your ankles under a sturdy object and lower your torso with a partner or band assistance. This is the closest bodyweight substitute.
- **Stability ball leg curl** — lie on your back, place your heels on a ball, and bridge your hips while curling the ball toward you. Beginner-friendly and home-gym compatible.
- **Sliding leg curl** — use sliders or a towel on a smooth floor for a similar hamstring-dominant movement.
- **Band-assisted GHR** — loop a resistance band under your chest and over a high anchor to reduce load on the way up.

Each of these trains the same fundamental pattern, so you can build strength and progress toward the full GHR over time.

## How to Perform the Glute-Ham Raise With Proper Form

**Setup:**
1. Adjust the footplate so your knees sit just behind the pad and your thighs rest firmly on it.
2. Secure your feet with heels pressed into the plate and toes relaxed.
3. Start with your body in a straight line from head to heels—hips extended, core braced.

**The lowering phase (the money phase):**
- Squeeze your glutes and keep your spine neutral—avoid arching your lower back.
- Lower your torso forward slowly, resisting gravity with your hamstrings. Aim for a 3–5 second descent.
- Keep your hips extended as long as possible; don't let them break early into a "pike."
- Lower only as far as you can control. For beginners, that may be just 20–30 degrees.

**The return phase:**
- Pull yourself back up by driving your heels into the plate and squeezing your hamstrings and glutes.
- If you can't complete the concentric on your own, push off the floor with your hands to assist—this is a legitimate training method, not cheating.

**Key form cues:**
- "Squeeze the glutes, protect the spine."
- "Heels down, hamstrings on."
- "Control the descent—that's where the work happens."

## Programming for Every Fitness Level

**Beginner (0–3 months):**
- Start with band-assisted GHRs or stability ball leg curls, 3 sets of 5–8 reps with a slow 4-second lower.
- Use hand assistance generously on the way up. The goal is controlled eccentrics, not ego.
- Train the GHR once or twice per week, ideally after your main squat or hinge movement.

**Intermediate (3–12 months):**
- Progress to unassisted eccentrics with hand-assisted concentrics, 3–4 sets of 5–10 reps.
- Add a 2-second pause at your lowest controllable position to build strength in lengthened ranges.
- Consider pairing the GHR with a hip-dominant lift (like Romanian deadlifts) for well-rounded posterior chain development.

**Advanced (12+ months):**
- Perform full, unassisted reps, 3–5 sets of 6–12 reps.
- Add load via a weight vest or plate held at the chest.
- Use tempo variations (e.g., 5-second lowers) or cluster sets to increase volume without sacrificing form.

**Realistic timelines:** Expect noticeable strength gains in 6–8 weeks and visible hamstring development in 3–4 months of consistent training. Full unassisted GHRs often take 6–12 months to achieve—and that's completely normal.

## Recovery, Nutrition, and Injury Prevention

The GHR is demanding on the hamstrings, so recovery deserves attention:

- **Allow 48–72 hours between hard GHR sessions.** Hamstrings respond well to frequency, but not to back-to-back heavy eccentric work.
- **Prioritize protein.** Aim for roughly 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kilogram of bodyweight daily to support muscle repair and growth.
- **Don't neglect carbohydrates.** Glycogen replenishment supports recovery from high-tension strength work.
- **Address soreness proactively.** Mild delayed-onset muscle soreness is normal; sharp pain, especially near the sit bone, warrants rest and assessment.
- **Warm up thoroughly.** A few sets of light leg curls, glute bridges, and dynamic hamstring swings prepare the tissue for eccentric loading.

**Safety note:** If you have a history of hamstring strain, start conservatively and consult a qualified professional before adding heavy eccentric work. Never chase range of motion you can't control—partial reps done well beat full reps done sloppily.

## The Bottom Line

The glute-ham raise is a rare exercise that builds muscle and reduces injury risk at the same time. Whether you're a beginner using band assistance or an advanced lifter loading full reps, the principles are the same: control the eccentric, keep your hips extended, and progress gradually. Add the GHR to your lower-body training once or twice a week, pair it with smart nutrition and recovery habits, and give it a few months. Your hamstrings—and your future self—will thank you.
