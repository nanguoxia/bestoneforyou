# Beyond the Gear: What We Can Learn from Rémi Bonnet’s Vertical Running Philosophy

When we look at elite mountain runners like Rémi Bonnet—the Swiss vertical running sensation known for his dominance in skyrunning and uphill races—it is tempting to focus solely on the "stuff." We see the carbon-plated trail shoes, the minimalist layers, and the high-end Tudor watch. But for the everyday fitness enthusiast, the real value lies not in the price tag of the gear, but in the physiological principles and training methodologies that allow athletes to conquer gravity.

Whether you are training for your first local 5K, a grueling obstacle course, or simply want to improve your cardiovascular health on the treadmill, the lessons from vertical running apply to everyone. Here is a breakdown of the training, nutrition, and recovery strategies inspired by the world’s best uphill runners, adapted for the rest of us.

## The Physiology of the Climb: Why Uphill is King

Vertical running (or power hiking) is one of the most efficient ways to build cardiovascular fitness while sparing the joints. Recent findings in exercise science suggest that uphill running significantly increases the activation of the gluteus maximus and hamstrings compared to flat running, while reducing the impact forces on the knees.

**The Data:** A 2022 study published in the *Journal of Sports Science and Medicine* found that incline treadmill walking or running at a 10–15% grade can increase caloric expenditure by up to 60% compared to flat running at the same speed. Furthermore, for those looking to improve VO2 max (a key marker of longevity), uphill intervals are highly effective because they force the heart to work against gravity without the high-speed mechanical stress of sprinting.

### Training Application: The "Vertical" Session
You don’t need a mountain to train like a skyrunner.
- **Beginners:** Start with "Power Hiking" on a treadmill at a 5–8% incline. Keep your heart rate in Zone 2 (conversational pace). Aim for 20–30 minutes.
- **Intermediate/Advanced:** Incorporate "Vertical Intervals." Warm up for 10 minutes. Then, run at a hard effort (Zone 4) uphill for 2 minutes, followed by 2 minutes of flat recovery jogging. Repeat 5–8 times.
- **Form Cue:** Shorten your stride. Overstriding on an incline wastes energy. Lean slightly *into* the hill from your ankles, not your waist, and drive your elbows back to power the legs.

## The Gear Philosophy: Minimalism and Function

Bonnet is known for carrying very little. For the recreational athlete, the takeaway is not to buy the most expensive watch, but to prioritize **function over fashion**.

**Footwear:** While elite runners use carbon-plated shoes for propulsion, most recreational runners benefit more from **stability and grip**. If you are running trails, prioritize a shoe with deep lugs (grip) and a rock plate (protection). If you are on a treadmill, a comfortable daily trainer is sufficient. Do not save your "race shoes" for training; save them for race day to avoid injury from lack of support.

**Layering:** The "Swiss Army Knife" approach to clothing is key. Bonnet relies on layers to regulate body temperature.
- **Actionable Tip:** Wear a moisture-wicking base layer (merino wool or synthetic). Avoid cotton, which holds sweat and chills you when you stop. If it is cold, add a lightweight windbreaker. You should feel slightly cold when you step outside; you will warm up within 10 minutes of climbing.

## Nutrition for the Uphill Effort

Climbing requires a massive amount of energy. Bonnet and other endurance athletes focus on **fueling early and often**.

- **Pre-Workout:** For efforts lasting over 60 minutes, consume 30–60g of carbohydrates per hour. This can be a banana, a sports drink, or an energy gel.
- **Hydration:** Dehydration impairs performance before you even feel thirsty. A good rule of thumb is 500ml (16oz) of water per hour of moderate exercise, adjusted for heat and sweat rate.
- **Recovery:** Within 30–60 minutes of finishing a hard vertical session, consume a combination of protein (20–30g) and carbohydrates to repair muscle damage and replenish glycogen. Chocolate milk is a scientifically backed, cost-effective recovery drink.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

Vertical running puts significant load on the Achilles tendon and calves. Bonnet’s ability to recover is as important as his ability to train.

**Safety Considerations:**
1.  **Eccentric Strength:** To prevent Achilles issues, perform "calf raises" with a slow, controlled lowering phase (3 seconds down, 1 second up).
2.  **Downhill Mechanics:** Running up is hard work; running down is where injuries happen. If you are trail running, shorten your stride on descents and keep your cadence high (around 170–180 steps per minute) to reduce braking forces on the knees.
3.  **The "Watch" Metric:** While Bonnet wears a Tudor, you don't need a luxury watch. However, using a basic heart rate monitor or smartwatch to track your **Resting Heart Rate (RHR)** is valuable. An elevated RHR (5–10 beats above normal) is a sign you need a rest day.

## Realistic Timelines for Progress

It is easy to get discouraged when comparing yourself to elites. Here is what you can realistically expect:

- **Beginner (0–3 months):** Expect significant improvements in walking pace and comfort on inclines. Your heart rate will drop during sub-maximal efforts as your stroke volume (heart efficiency) improves.
- **Intermediate (3–6 months):** You will notice your ability to sustain running (not just walking) on hills increases. You may see a 5–10% improvement in your 5K time if you include one hill session per week.
- **Advanced (6+ months):** Improvements become marginal. Focus shifts to periodization—building a base in the off-season and peaking for specific races.

## The Coaching Perspective: Consistency Over Intensity

The biggest lesson from elite athletes is not their gear, but their consistency. "Thebest training plan is the one you can stick to," says Coach Sarah Jones, a certified strength and conditioning specialist. "For vertical running, start by adding just one hill repeat to your weekly run. Master the form, listen to your body, and gradually increase the volume."

**Final Takeaway:** You don't need a Tudor watch to run up a mountain. You need a pair of shoes that fit, a plan that progresses gradually, and the discipline to fuel and recover properly. Whether you are power-hiking a local trail or grinding on a treadmill incline, the principles of vertical training—strength, efficiency, and endurance—are universal.
