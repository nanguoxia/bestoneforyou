# The Secret Lessons About Gels Every Marathoner Learns Eventually

## A time-saving guide to picking your perfect race food

Every marathoner has a story about "that gel." Maybe it was the one that tasted like salted cardboard at mile 18. Maybe it was the one that sat heavy in the stomach and refused to move. Or maybe—if you're lucky—it was the one that delivered a perfectly timed surge exactly when the wall loomed.

Here's the truth most runners learn the hard way: **race nutrition is not a detail. It's a discipline.** And like any discipline, it rewards preparation and punishes improvisation.

This guide will help you cut through the marketing noise and build a fueling strategy that actually works for your body, your pace, and your race-day goals.

---

## Why Gels Matter More Than You Think

During a marathon, your body relies on two primary fuel sources: glycogen (stored carbohydrate) and fat. Even the leanest, most efficient runner carries only about 1,800 to 2,000 calories of glycogen—enough for roughly 90 to 120 minutes of hard running.

After that, performance declines sharply unless you replace carbohydrate on the move.

Research consistently shows that endurance athletes who consume **60 to 90 grams of carbohydrate per hour** during events lasting more than two hours perform significantly better than those who consume less. A 2018 review in *Sports Medicine* found that carbohydrate intake during prolonged exercise can improve performance by 2 to 3 percent—which, for a 3:30 marathoner, translates to roughly 4 to 6 minutes.

That's the difference between a personal best and a disappointing day.

Gels exist because they're portable, calorie-dense, and fast-absorbing. But not all gels are created equal, and not every runner tolerates them the same way.

---

## The Three Types of Gels (and Who They Suit)

### 1. Traditional Gels (Single-Source Carbohydrate)

These are your classic maltodextrin or glucose-based gels. They typically deliver 20 to 25 grams of carbohydrate per packet and are absorbed quickly.

**Best for:** Beginners, shorter races, and runners with sensitive stomachs who want a simple, predictable option.

**Examples:** GU Original, Clif Shot, PowerBar Gel.

**Coaching tip:** Take these with 4 to 6 ounces of water. Never take a gel with a sports drink—you'll spike your carbohydrate concentration and risk gastrointestinal distress.

### 2. Multi-Transportable Carbohydrate Gels

These contain a blend of glucose and fructose, typically in a 2:1 or 1:0.8 ratio. This matters because glucose and fructose use different intestinal transporters, allowing your body to absorb more total carbohydrate per hour.

**Best for:** Intermediate to advanced runners targeting 60 to 90 grams of carbohydrate per hour, especially in longer races.

**Examples:** Maurten Gel 100, SiS Go Isotonic, Enervit Carbo Gel.

**Data point:** Studies show that 2:1 glucose-to-fructose blends can increase carbohydrate oxidation rates by up to 40 percent compared to single-source glucose.

### 3. Real-Food and Liquid Gels

These use whole-food ingredients or thinner, drink-like consistency. They're often easier on the stomach but may deliver fewer calories per ounce.

**Best for:** Runners with gastrointestinal sensitivity, ultra runners, and those who struggle with traditional gel textures.

**Examples:** Spring Energy, Huma Chia, Untapped Maple.

**Alternative:** If you can't tolerate any gel, consider chews, jelly beans, or a concentrated carbohydrate drink mix. The best fuel is the one you can actually consume.

## How to Find Your Gel (Without Wasting a Marathon)

### Step 1: Start in Training, Not on Race Day

The golden rule: **never try anything new on race day.** Your long runs are your laboratory.

Begin experimenting 8 to 12 weeks before your goal race. Use your weekly long run to test one gel type at a time. Note:

- Taste and texture
- Ease of opening and consuming while running
- Digestive response (bloating, cramping, urgency)
- Energy stability over the next 30 to 60 minutes

### Step 2: Practice Your Race-Day Timing

Simulate race conditions. If you plan to take a gel every 35 to 40 minutes, do that in training. Your gut is trainable—but it needs repetition.

**Beginner timeline:** Start with one gel at 45 minutes, then one every 45 to 60 minutes.

**Intermediate timeline:** One gel every 35 to 40 minutes, aiming for 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour.

**Advanced timeline:** One gel every 25 to 30 minutes, aiming for 75 to 90 grams per hour, often combined with a carbohydrate drink.

### Step 3: Hydrate Strategically

Gels need water to be absorbed efficiently. A good rule: **4 to 8 ounces of water with each gel.** If you're using an isotonic gel (like SiS), you may need less.

**Safety note:** Taking gels without adequate water can pull fluid into your stomach and cause nausea. Taking them with too much can cause sloshing and discomfort. Practice both.

---

## The Gut-Training Protocol

Your gastrointestinal system adapts to carbohydrate intake during exercise. A 2020 study in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* found that runners who practiced gut training for two weeks increased their carbohydrate absorption efficiency and reduced gastrointestinal symptoms.

**How to do it:**

1. **Weeks 1–2:** Consume 30 grams of carbohydrate per hour during long runs.
2. **Weeks 3–4:** Increase to 45 grams per hour.
3. **Weeks 5–6:** Increase to 60 grams per hour.
4. **Weeks 7–8:** If tolerated, push to 75–90 grams per hour.

This is not about toughness. It's about biology. Your gut has transporters, and they respond to training like any other system.

## Practical Tips from the Coaching Trenches

**Tip 1: Pre-open your gels.** Fumbling with packaging at mile 20 wastes energy and focus. Tear the top before you start running, or use a gel flask.

**Tip 2: Alternate flavors.** Flavor fatigue is real. Carrying two or three different flavors can keep you motivated to fuel.

**Tip 3: Caffeine timing matters.** Caffeine gels (typically 25–50 mg) can boost performance, but take them early—around 45 to 60 minutes before you need the lift. Avoid caffeine in the final hour if you're sensitive to sleep disruption post-race.

**Tip 4: Don't neglect electrolytes.** Gels provide carbohydrate, not sodium. In hot conditions, pair gels with an electrolyte drink or salt capsules. Sweat rates vary widely—some runners lose over 1,000 mg of sodium per liter of sweat.

**Tip 5: Have a backup plan.** Aid stations may not carry your gel. Carry your own, and know what's available on course. If you're traveling, pack extra.

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention Considerations

Race fueling doesn't end at the finish line. Within 30 to 60 minutes post-race, aim for **1.0 to 1.2 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight** plus **20 to 30 grams of protein** to kickstart glycogen resynthesis and muscle repair.

**Injury prevention note:** Poor fueling increases injury risk. When glycogen runs low, form deteriorates, fatigue accumulates, and compensatory movement patterns emerge. Proper nutrition is a protective factor, not just a performance enhancer.

**Recovery weeks:** After a marathon, reduce training volume by 50 to 70 percent for 2 to 3 weeks. Continue prioritizing carbohydrate and protein. Sleep 8 to 9 hours per night. Consider a sports massage or gentle yoga to restore mobility.

---

## Realistic Timelines for Results

- **2–4 weeks:** You'll notice improved energy stability during long runs if you're fueling consistently.
- **6–8 weeks:** Gut tolerance improves. You can handle higher carbohydrate intakes with fewer symptoms.
- **One full training cycle (12–16 weeks):** Your race-day fueling becomes automatic. You'll know your gels, your timing, and your hydration strategy without thinking.

There are no shortcuts. But there is a system. And it works.

---

## The Bottom Line

The secret lesson every marathoner learns is this: **your race is only as good as your fueling.** Gels are not a crutch. They're a tool. And like any tool, they require practice, precision, and a willingness to adapt.

Start early. Test often. Train your gut. Respect your body's signals. And on race day, trust the work you've done.

Your perfect gel is out there. Go find it—one long run at a time.
