# Training After 50: What Actually Works for Building Muscle and Staying Lean

## Lessons from a 53-Year-Old Who's Still Making Gains

Bryan Krahn is 53 years old and lean enough to see his abs. That sentence shouldn't be remarkable—except that it runs counter to everything most men his age have been told about aging, hormones, and the inevitable decline of the physique after 50.

Krahn, a strength coach and writer who has spent decades in the trenches of the iron game, isn't selling a secret. He's sharing a set of adjustments—small, deliberate tweaks to training, nutrition, and recovery—that allow him to keep building muscle and staying lean well into his fifties. And the science increasingly backs up his approach.

Here's what the research says, what Krahn does differently, and how you can apply it regardless of whether you're 35, 53, or 65.

---

## The Science: Muscle After 50 Is Not a Lost Cause

The narrative that muscle mass falls off a cliff after 50 is oversimplified. Research consistently shows that adults can build muscle at any age—the question is how much and how fast.

A landmark 2019 study in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* found that older adults (average age 70) who performed resistance training three times per week for 12 weeks gained lean mass comparable to younger participants when the training stimulus was adequate. The key phrase: *when the training stimulus was adequate.*

Other findings worth knowing:

- **Sarcopenia accelerates after 60**, but its roots begin in the 40s. The earlier you train, the more you preserve.
- **Testosterone declines roughly 1% per year after 30**, but resistance training and adequate sleep can blunt the functional impact.
- **Protein synthesis becomes less efficient with age**—a phenomenon called anabolic resistance—meaning older lifters may need more protein per meal to trigger the same muscle-building response as a 25-year-old.
- **Recovery capacity drops**, making sleep, stress management, and smart programming non-negotiable rather than optional.

In other words, the rules don't change after 50. The *margins* do.

## The Krahn Approach: What Changes After 50

Krahn's philosophy boils down to a single principle: **train hard, recover harder, and stop chasing numbers that belonged to your 30-year-old self.**

### 1. Fewer junk sets, more quality reps

Where a 25-year-old might grind through 25 sets per muscle group per week, Krahn advocates for **10–16 hard sets per muscle group**, taken close to failure but not beyond it. The research on volume and hypertrophy supports this: more isn't better once you hit the effective range—it's just more fatigue.

**Actionable tip:** If you're over 50, cut your weekly volume by 20–30% from what you did in your 30s and put that energy into intensity of effort. Two to three working sets per exercise, taken to 1–2 reps shy of failure, is plenty for most lifters.

### 2. Prioritize the big rocks—but respect the joints

Squats, deadlifts, presses, and rows remain the foundation. But Krahn swaps in variations that spare the lower back and shoulders:

- **Trap bar deadlifts** instead of straight-bar deadlifts
- **Safety bar or front squats** instead of back squats
- **Dumbbell or machine presses** instead of barbell bench when shoulders complain
- **Chest-supported rows** instead of bent-over rows

**Form cue:** For any pressing movement, keep shoulder blades retracted and depressed—think "proud chest, shoulders in back pockets." This protects the rotator cuff and improves force transfer.

### 3. Train each muscle twice per week

Frequency beats volume for older lifters because protein synthesis stays elevated for a shorter window after training. Hitting each muscle group twice weekly with moderate volume produces better gains than one weekly blitz.

### 4. Take the deload seriously

Krahn deloads every 4–6 weeks—not because he's weak, but because recovery debt compounds faster after 50. A deload means reducing volume by 40–50% while keeping intensity moderate.

**Realistic timeline:** Expect visible strength gains in 6–8 weeks and noticeable physique changes in 12–16 weeks. After 50, patience isn't a virtue—it's a requirement.

## Nutrition: The Non-Negotiables

### Protein: More than you think

Current recommendations for older adults suggest **1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day** (roughly 0.7–1.0 g per pound). For a 180-pound man, that's 130–180 grams daily.

Just as important: **distribute it across 3–4 meals**, with 30–50 grams per meal. A 2020 review in *Nutrients* found that older adults need a higher per-meal protein dose to maximally stimulate muscle protein synthesis.

**Practical sources:** Greek yogurt, eggs, chicken, lean beef, fish, whey protein, cottage cheese. A whey shake post-workout is convenient but not magic—total daily intake matters most.

### Calories: Slightly above maintenance on training days

Krahn doesn't do aggressive bulks. After 50, excess calories tend to land as fat more readily due to hormonal shifts and reduced NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis). A **200–300 calorie surplus on training days** and maintenance on rest days is a sustainable approach for lean gains.

### The supplements worth considering

- **Creatine monohydrate (3–5 g daily):** The most evidence-backed supplement for strength and lean mass in older adults. A 2021 meta-analysis confirmed benefits for adults over 50.
- **Vitamin D (1,000–2,000 IU daily):** Supports bone health, immune function, and testosterone.
- **Omega-3s (1–2 g EPA/DHA):** May reduce inflammation and support joint health.
- **Magnesium (200–400 mg before bed):** Supports sleep quality and recovery.

Skip the rest unless a blood test says otherwise.

## Recovery and Sleep: The Real Secret

Krahn is blunt about this: **sleep is the best anabolic you'll ever take.**

Research shows that sleeping less than 6 hours per night can reduce testosterone by 10–15% and impair muscle protein synthesis. For older lifters, the stakes are higher.

**Actionable sleep habits:**
- Aim for 7–9 hours, with a consistent bedtime and wake time.
- Keep the room cool (65–68°F / 18–20°C) and dark.
- Avoid alcohol within 3 hours of bed—it fragments REM sleep.
- Limit caffeine after 2 p.m.
- Consider a 20-minute nap if night sleep is chronically short.

**Injury prevention:** Warm up with 5–10 minutes of light cardio and dynamic stretching. Never skip it. After 50, cold muscles tear more easily. And if something hurts sharply, stop—sharp pain is not "weakness leaving the body."

## Adjusting for Different Fitness Levels

**Beginners (any age):**
- Start with 2 full-body sessions per week, 6–8 exercises, 2 sets each.
- Focus on machines and bodyweight movements first.
- Master form before adding load.

**Intermediate (1–3 years training):**
- Move to an upper/lower split, 3–4 sessions per week.
- Add 1–2 sets per exercise as recovery allows.
- Introduce intensity techniques (drop sets, rest-pause) sparingly.

**Advanced (3+ years):**
- Periodize: 4–6 weeks accumulation, 1 week deload.
- Use RPE (rate of perceived exertion) to autoregulate—stop at RPE 8–9, not 10.
- Consider working with a coach to audit your programming.

**Equipment alternatives:**
- No barbell? Use dumbbells, kettlebells, or resistance bands.
- No gym? Bodyweight progressions (push-up variations, split squats, inverted rows) build real muscle.
- Traveling? A single adjustable dumbbell and a pull-up bar cover 80% of needs.

## Coach's Corner: Practical Tips That Matter

1. **Track your lifts.** What gets measured gets managed. A simple notebook or app works.
2. **Eat protein at breakfast.** Most people skimp early and overdo it at dinner. Flip that.
3. **Walk daily.** 7,000–10,000 steps supports recovery and cardiovascular health without eating into recovery.
4. **Don't compare yourself to 25-year-olds.** Compare to the version of you from six months ago.
5. **Get bloodwork annually.** Testosterone, vitamin D, lipids, and thyroid—know your numbers.
6. **Hire a coach if you're stuck.** A fresh set of eyes beats another year of guesswork.

---

## The Bottom Line

Bryan Krahn's physique at 53 isn't an accident. It's the product of decades of consistency, intelligent programming, and a willingness to adapt. The science is clear: muscle is buildable and maintainable after 50. What changes is the margin for error.

Train hard. Eat enough protein. Sleep like it's your job. Deload before you break. And measure progress in quarters, not weeks.

You don't have to be 25 to be ripped. You just have to be smart.
