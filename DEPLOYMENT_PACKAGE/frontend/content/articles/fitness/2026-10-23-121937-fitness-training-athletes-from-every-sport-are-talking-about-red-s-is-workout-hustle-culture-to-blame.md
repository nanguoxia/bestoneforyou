# Athletes From Every Sport Are Talking About 'RED-S.' Is Workout Hustle Culture to Blame?

## What Is RED-S?

For decades, the term "Female Athlete Triad" dominated sports medicine, describing a cluster of interrelated issues — low energy availability, menstrual dysfunction, and low bone mineral density — affecting female endurance athletes. But in 2014, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) introduced a broader, more accurate framework: **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S)**.

RED-S is a syndrome that occurs when an athlete's caloric intake is insufficient to cover both the energy demands of training and the basic physiological functions required to keep the body healthy — a state known as **low energy availability (LEA)**. When the body is chronically under-fueled, it makes a series of physiological "triage" decisions, suppressing non-essential systems to conserve energy for survival.

**The systems RED-S can impair include:**

- **Endocrine function** — disrupted hormones, including insulin, cortisol, growth hormone, and sex hormones
- **Metabolic rate** — a downregulated resting metabolism as the body defends against energy deficit
- **Menstrual function** — irregular or absent periods (amenorrhea) in females; suppressed testosterone in males
- **Bone health** — reduced bone mineral density, increasing stress fracture risk
- **Immune function** — more frequent illness and slower recovery
- **Cardiovascular health** — altered heart rate variability and blood pressure regulation
- **Psychological health** — mood disturbances, depression, anxiety, and disordered eating patterns
- **Performance** — impaired endurance, strength gains, coordination, and recovery

Crucially, RED-S is **not gender-specific**. The IOC's 2023 consensus update explicitly emphasized that male athletes are affected too, with research suggesting prevalence rates in male athletes ranging from **15–30%** in some sport populations — a figure that was largely overlooked for years.

## Is Hustle Culture the Culprit?

The short answer: hustle culture is a significant contributor, but the condition is multifactorial.

Modern fitness culture glorifies "no days off," "earning your calories," fasted training, and pushing through fatigue as badges of honor. Wearable technology and social media amplify this — apps that display calorie deficits in red, leaderboards that reward more training volume, and influencers who equate rest days with weakness.

According to the **IOC 2023 consensus statement**, the primary driver of RED-S is **unintentional** — not deliberate restriction. Many athletes simply don't understand how much they need to eat to support their training load. A runner who adds a 40-mile week without adjusting their diet, or a recreational CrossFit athlete who starts a 75 Hard-style challenge while training six days a week, can slide into LEA without ever intending to under-eat.

That said, intentional restriction and disordered eating behaviors are real and often co-occur with RED-S, particularly in endurance sports, weight-class sports (rowing, wrestling, martial arts), and aesthetic sports (gymnastics, figure skating). The line between "discipline" and dysfunction can blur quickly in environments that reward leanness and training volume above all else.

**Key data points from recent research:**

- A 2022 systematic review in *Sports Medicine* found LEA prevalence as high as **40–80%** in some elite female athlete cohorts, depending on the assessment method used.
- The IOC's 2023 update estimated that RED-S affects **both male and female athletes across all sports**, with endurance and weight-sensitive sports showing the highest risk.
- A 2021 study in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* found that athletes with LEA had **significantly higher rates of stress fractures** — a finding with direct implications for recreational athletes ramping up training for events like marathons or obstacle races.

## Recognizing the Warning Signs

Because RED-S develops gradually, it's easy to dismiss early symptoms as normal training fatigue. Watch for these red flags in yourself or your training partners:

**Performance and training signs:**
- Stalled progress despite consistent training
- Persistent fatigue or "dead legs" that don't resolve with rest
- Increased injury frequency, especially stress fractures or tendon issues
- Reduced power or endurance in sessions you previously handled comfortably
- Poor recovery between sessions

**Physiological signs:**
- Unexplained weight loss or, paradoxically, weight gain (metabolic adaptation)
- Frequent illness or slow-healing wounds
- Irregular, missed, or absent periods (in females)
- Low libido or morning erections (in males)
- Feeling cold frequently, especially in extremities
- Reduced resting heart rate and blood pressure (in severe cases)
- Hair thinning or dry skin

**Psychological and behavioral signs:**
- Preoccupation with food, weight, or body composition
- Anxiety around missed workouts or rest days
- Guilt when eating "unplanned" foods
- Withdrawing from social situations involving food
- Mood swings, irritability, or low mood
- Rigid, rule-bound eating patterns

If several of these resonate, it's worth a conversation with a sports medicine physician or registered sports dietitian — ideally one with experience in RED-S screening tools like the **LEAF-Q** (Low Energy Availability in Females Questionnaire) or the **RED-S CAT** (Clinical Assessment Tool).

## Practical Fueling Strategies to Prevent RED-S

The core fix for RED-S is straightforward in principle — **match energy intake to energy expenditure** — though it's often difficult in practice. Here are actionable strategies for different training levels.

### For Beginners

- **Don't train fasted for high-intensity sessions.** A 2021 study in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* found that fasted training blunted performance and increased cortisol responses compared to fed training. Eat a small carbohydrate-containing snack (30–60g carbs) 60–90 minutes before hard sessions.
- **Aim for 20–30g of protein per meal**, spread across 3–4 meals. This supports muscle repair without requiring obsessive tracking.
- **Treat rest days as training days for nutrition.** Many beginners unintentionally under-eat on recovery days because they're less hungry — but recovery is when adaptation happens.
- **Use simple hunger-fullness cues** rather than apps. If you're hungry 30 minutes after a meal, you likely didn't eat enough.

### For Intermediate and Advanced Athletes

- **Calculate a rough energy availability (EA) floor.** EA = (energy intake − exercise energy expenditure) / fat-free mass (kg). Research suggests **EA below 30 kcal/kg fat-free mass** is a threshold for LEA, while **45 kcal/kg FFM** is generally considered the healthy lower bound for female athletes (male thresholds are less firmly established but likely similar). This isn't a prescription to track obsessively — it's a sanity check.
- **Periodize nutrition with training.** On high-volume days, increase carbohydrate intake by 1–2g/kg body weight. A 2022 review in *Nutrients* found that carbohydrate availability directly influences bone turnover markers — relevant to RED-S risk.
- **Prioritize carbohydrate around key sessions.** Pre-session: 1–2g/kg carbs 2–3 hours before; during sessions over 90 minutes: 30–60g carbs per hour; post-session: 1g/kg carbs plus 0.3g/kg protein within 60 minutes.
- **Monitor for "relative" under-fueling in high-volume blocks.** If your training volume increases 20% but your intake stays flat, you're creating a deficit. Adjust intentionally.

### For All Levels

- **Don't demonize carbohydrates.** Low-carb and keto approaches can be appropriate for some athletes in some contexts, but combining them with high training volume is a recipe for LEA and RED-S risk.
- **Alcohol, caffeine, and high-fiber diets can suppress appetite** — be mindful that these don't crowd out needed calories.
- **If you're tracking macros, set protein and carbs first**, then fill remaining calories with fat. Tracking should be a tool, not a tyrant.

## Training Adjustments and Recovery

Nutrition is the cornerstone, but training management matters too.

- **Follow the 80/20 rule:** 80% of training should be low-to-moderate intensity, 20% high intensity. This is a proven framework (popularized by Matt Fitzgerald and supported by research from Stephen Seiler) that reduces total stress and supports recovery.
- **Take a deload week every 4–6 weeks** — reduce volume by 40–50% while maintaining some intensity. This is standard programming for a reason: it allows adaptation to catch up with stress.
- **Sleep 7–9 hours per night.** Sleep restriction amplifies LEA's effects on hormones and recovery. A 2021 study in *Sleep* found that athletes sleeping under 7 hours had significantly higher injury rates.
- **Use HRV and resting heart rate as early warning systems.** A rising resting HR and falling HRV over several days can signal under-recovery — often a precursor to LEA symptoms.
- **Schedule at least one full rest day per week** — and don't "make up" for it later.

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

You don't need a gym to train well, but you do need to fuel for whatever you do.

- **Home training:** Bodyweight circuits and resistance bands can be surprisingly metabolically demanding. Don't assume low-equipment training means low energy needs — a 30-minute band circuit can burn 250–350 kcal.
- **Outdoor training:** Heat and altitude increase energy expenditure and fluid needs. Add 500–750ml of fluid per hour in hot conditions and increase carbohydrate intake modestly.
- **Travel:** Pack portable protein sources (jerky, protein powder, shelf-stable milk) and don't skip meals to "save calories" before a workout — that's a classic LEA trap.
- **Low-impact alternatives when injured:** Swimming, cycling, and rowing maintain aerobic fitness while reducing impact stress. But remember: **injury is not a reason to under-eat** — healing requires energy.

## Coaching Perspectives and Personal Insights

From a coaching standpoint, one of the most useful shifts is reframing rest and food as **performance tools rather than concessions**. Athletes who fuel well and rest adequately consistently outperform those who train more and eat less — the research on LEA and performance decline (reduced endurance, strength, and coordination) is unambiguous.

A few practical coaching cues that work well:

- **"Eat for the work you're about to do, not the work you wish you did."** Match intake to actual training load.
- **"If you're not recovering, you're not training — you're surviving."** Recovery is when adaptation happens; under-fueling turns training into damage.
- **"Track your period like you track your splits."** Menstrual cycle tracking is one of the most reliable early indicators of LEA in female athletes.
- **"Strength is a signal of health, not just performance."** Losing strength or power despite training is a red flag, not a badge of honor.

## Realistic Timelines

Recovery from RED-S is not overnight. Depending on severity:

- **Mild LEA (unintentional, early stage):** Improvements in energy, mood, and performance may appear within **2–4 weeks** of adequate fueling.
- **Moderate RED-S (hormonal disruption, bone density changes):** Expect **3–6 months** for hormonal normalization and gradual performance recovery. Bone density improvements may take **6–12 months** or longer.
- **Severe RED-S (clinical diagnosis, stress fractures, disordered eating):** Requires multidisciplinary care (physician, dietitian, therapist) and may take **12–24 months** for full recovery. In some cases, bone density may never fully return to baseline.

The earlier RED-S is identified and addressed, the better the prognosis. This is why awareness — and pushing back against hustle culture's normalization of chronic under-fueling — matters so much.

## The Bottom Line

RED-S is a serious, under-recognized condition that affects athletes of all genders, sports, and levels. Hustle culture contributes by glorifying training volume and caloric restriction, but the most common cause is simple: **not eating enough to support training demands**, often unintentionally.

The fix is not exotic. Eat enough, eat carbohydrate, fuel around training, sleep, rest, and monitor your body's signals. If you suspect RED-S in yourself or an athlete you coach, seek qualified medical and nutritional support — and remember that backing off is sometimes the most performance-enhancing thing you can do.
