# The 6 Best Training Tools for Grip Strength, Tested by Fitness Experts

## Give your hands a break from plateaus with these must-have strength builders.

---

Grip strength might be the most overlooked performance marker in fitness. Yet the research keeps stacking up: it predicts everything from deadlift maxes to all-cause mortality. A 2022 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* found that grip strength correlates strongly with overall muscular strength and is a reliable indicator of training readiness. Meanwhile, landmark data from the PURE study (published in *The Lancet*) tracked nearly 140,000 adults across 17 countries and found that each 5 kg decrease in grip strength was associated with a 16% higher risk of all-cause mortality.

Translation: your hands, wrists, and forearms deserve dedicated work — not just whatever residual stimulus they get from pulling movements.

But here's the catch. Grip training is notoriously easy to overtrain, hard to program, and riddled with junk equipment. So we tested six of the most popular grip and forearm training tools on the market, evaluated them across beginner-to-advanced applications, and broke down exactly how to use each one without wrecking your elbows.

Whether you train in a fully stocked gym, a garage setup, or a hotel room with a single resistance band, there's an option here for you.

---

## How We Tested

Each tool was evaluated over a 12-week block by testers ranging from novice lifters (less than one year of training) to advanced athletes (five-plus years). We assessed:

- **Effectiveness** — measurable improvement in crushing, pinching, and supporting grip
- **Durability** — performance after 500+ repetitions
- **Versatility** — applicability across strength, climbing, and sport-specific goals
- **Value** — cost relative to results
- **Safety profile** — injury risk with improper use

**Important safety note up front:** Grip training loads the tendons of the fingers, wrists, and elbows — tissues that adapt far more slowly than muscle. Tendons respond to load over months, not weeks. If you feel sharp pain in the elbow (especially the inner elbow, where medial epicondylitis or "golfer's elbow" develops), back off immediately. Start with two grip sessions per week, never on consecutive days, and progress load by no more than 5–10% weekly.

## 1. Best Overall: Adjustable Hand Gripper

**Best for:** All levels | **Price range:** $20–$40

A quality adjustable gripper lets you dial resistance from roughly 20 lb to 150 lb, making it the single most versatile entry point into grip training. Beginners can start at a resistance they can close for 8–12 clean reps; advanced lifters can work heavy singles.

**How to program it:**
- **Beginners:** 2 sets of 10–12 reps per hand, twice weekly. Focus on a full range of motion — open the hand completely between reps.
- **Intermediate:** 3–4 sets of 8–10 reps, adding a 2-second hold at full closure.
- **Advanced:** Work up to heavy singles or triples, then finish with a high-rep burnout set.

**Form cue:** Keep the wrist neutral — don't let it curl inward or extend back. The movement should come from the fingers and forearm, not the wrist joint.

**Coaching tip:** Train both hands equally, even if one is dominant. Asymmetrical grip strength is a common contributor to elbow and shoulder issues down the line.

---

## 2. Best for Supporting Grip: Loading Pin + Weight Plates

**Best for:** Intermediate to advanced | **Price range:** $30–$60 (plus plates)

If you want to train the exact quality that limits your deadlift — supporting grip — a loading pin is the closest thing to sport-specific. You load plates onto the pin, attach a handle, and hold for time.

**How to program it:**
- **Beginners:** Start with a light load (roughly 40–50% of your deadlift working weight) and hold for 20–30 seconds. Two holds per session.
- **Intermediate:** 3 sets of 15–20 second holds at 60–70% of deadlift working weight.
- **Advanced:** Heavy holds for max time (aim 10–15 seconds), or progress to one-hand holds.

**Safety cue:** Set the pin down under control. Dropping it from height is how people break toes and crack garage floors.

**Timeline reality check:** Supporting grip endurance improves relatively quickly — expect noticeable carryover to your deadlift within 4–6 weeks. Peak crushing strength takes longer, often 8–12 weeks of consistent work.

## 3. Best for Pinch Strength: Pinch Blocks

**Best for:** Intermediate to advanced | **Price range:** $25–$50

Pinch grip is the forgotten child of hand strength, but it matters for rock climbers, gymnasts, and anyone who's ever fumbled a heavy plate. Pinch blocks come in varying widths, and the width changes the difficulty dramatically.

**How to program it:**
- Start with a wide block (3–4 inches) and a light load. Hold for 15–20 seconds.
- Progress by narrowing the block or adding weight.
- 3 sets per hand, twice weekly.

**Coaching tip:** Chalk helps, but don't rely on it as a crutch. Train some sets without chalk to build genuine skin-and-tissue resilience.

**Beginner alternative:** A stack of books or a thick textbook works as a makeshift pinch block. Not ideal, but functional.

---

## 4. Best for Forearm Hypertrophy: Wrist Roller

**Best for:** Beginners to intermediate | **Price range:** $15–$35

The wrist roller is old-school, and for good reason — it delivers a brutal forearm pump and builds wrist flexion/extension strength through a full range. It's also one of the most accessible tools for home trainees.

**How to program it:**
- Attach a light plate (5–10 lb for beginners). Roll the weight up and down, controlling the eccentric.
- 2–3 sets, twice weekly.
- **Advanced:** Increase load and add a tempo — 3 seconds up, 3 seconds down.

**Safety cue:** Don't let the weight free-fall on the eccentric. That's the fastest route to a wrist strain. Control is the whole point.

**Practical tip:** If you don't own a wrist roller, a PVC pipe, a length of rope, and a carabiner cost under $10 total and work identically.

## 5. Best Bodyweight Option: Dead Hangs and Towel Hangs

**Best for:** All levels | **Price range:** $0 (pull-up bar required)

No equipment budget? No problem. Dead hangs are one of the most effective and joint-friendly grip builders available, and they double as a shoulder decompression tool.

**How to program it:**
- **Beginners:** 3 sets of 20–30 second hangs. If bodyweight is too much, keep feet on the ground and offload partially.
- **Intermediate:** 3 sets of 45–60 seconds.
- **Advanced:** Towel hangs (drape a towel over the bar and grip the ends) for 3 sets of 20–30 seconds, or add weight via a dip belt.

**Safety cue:** Keep the shoulders slightly engaged — don't hang in a fully passive, slumped position for long durations, especially if you have a history of shoulder instability.

**Recovery note:** Hanging is low-impact but still loads the finger flexor tendons. If your fingers feel achy the day after, reduce volume by half for the following session.

---

## 6. Best for Climbers and Sport-Specific Athletes: Hangboard

**Best for:** Intermediate to advanced only | **Price range:** $80–$150

Hangboards are the gold standard for climbing-specific finger strength, but they carry the highest injury risk of anything on this list. The finger pulleys and tendons are exposed to extreme loads, and novices who jump in too fast are the ones who end up with pulley injuries that sideline them for months.

**How to program it:**
- **Do not start hangboarding until you have at least one year of consistent climbing or grip training.** This is a near-universal coaching recommendation.
- Begin with large edges (20mm+) and bodyweight, 6–10 second hangs, 3–5 sets, twice weekly.
- Progress over months, not weeks.

**Safety cue:** Never hangboard when fatigued or cold. Warm up thoroughly — light climbing or easy hangs for 10+ minutes before loading the fingers.

## Nutrition and Recovery for Grip Training

Grip training is tendon-dominant, and tendons need specific support:

- **Protein:** Aim for 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight daily to support connective tissue repair. Collagen synthesis benefits from adequate dietary protein plus vitamin C.
- **Collagen and vitamin C:** Some evidence suggests 15 g of collagen hydrolysate with 50 mg of vitamin C, taken 30–60 minutes before loading, may support tendon adaptation. The research is still emerging, but the risk is low and the potential benefit is reasonable.
- **Hydration:** Dehydrated tendons are stiffer and more injury-prone. Don't train grip in a depleted state.
- **Sleep:** Most tendon and muscle repair happens during sleep. Seven to nine hours is the target.

**Recovery rule of thumb:** Grip training should never be stacked directly before heavy pulling sessions. Do it after your main lifts, or on a separate day, so your hands aren't fried when you need them for deadlifts or rows.

## Realistic Timelines: What to Actually Expect

| Goal | Realistic Timeline |
|---|---|
| Noticeable crushing grip improvement | 4–6 weeks |
| Supporting grip carryover to deadlift | 4–8 weeks |
| Significant pinch strength gains | 8–12 weeks |
| Tendon resilience and injury resistance | 3–6 months |

If a program promises dramatic grip gains in two weeks, it's selling you something. Connective tissue doesn't work that way.

---

## The Bottom Line

Grip strength is a legitimate, measurable, health-relevant fitness quality — not a party trick. The best tool is the one you'll actually use consistently. Start with an adjustable gripper or dead hangs if you're new. Add a loading pin or pinch block as you progress. Respect the tendons, progress slowly, and treat grip work as a long game.

Your hands carry everything else. Train them like it matters — because it does.
