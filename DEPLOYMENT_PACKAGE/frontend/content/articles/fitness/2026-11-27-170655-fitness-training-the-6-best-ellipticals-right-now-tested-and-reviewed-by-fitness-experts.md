# The 6 Best Ellipticals Right Now, Tested and Reviewed by Fitness Experts

Your elliptical routine doesn't need to be limited to the fitness club. In fact, for millions of home exercisers, the elliptical trainer has become the quiet workhorse of the home gym—low-impact, joint-friendly, and surprisingly versatile. According to market research from the Sports & Fitness Industry Association (SFIA), home fitness equipment sales surged dramatically after 2020 and have remained elevated, with cardio machines like ellipticals and treadmills accounting for a substantial share of household fitness purchases. Meanwhile, a 2023 survey from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) ranked wearable technology and home-based exercise among the top fitness trends for the year—confirming that the "gym in the garage" isn't going anywhere.

But with dozens of models flooding the market—from budget-friendly folding units to commercial-grade machines with immersive screens—choosing the right elliptical can feel overwhelming. That's why we tested and evaluated the leading options across price points, examining stride length, resistance systems, build quality, and real-world usability.

Below, we break down the six best ellipticals available right now, offer expert-backed training advice for maximizing your machine, and share the form cues, recovery strategies, and nutrition principles that help you get results safely—whether you're stepping onto an elliptical for the first time or you're a seasoned athlete cross-training around a running injury.

## Why the Elliptical Deserves a Place in Your Routine

Before diving into the reviews, it's worth understanding what the elliptical actually does well—and where it falls short.

**The science-backed benefits:**

- **Low impact, high reward.** A landmark study published in the *Journal of Sports Science and Medicine* found that elliptical training produces significantly lower impact forces on the knees and ankles compared to treadmill running, while still elevating heart rate to levels consistent with moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise. For people managing joint pain, recovering from injury, or carrying excess body weight, this makes the elliptical an accessible entry point to cardio.
- **Caloric expenditure is comparable to running.** Research from the American Council on Exercise (ACE) found that elliptical exercise can burn roughly 8–11 calories per minute at moderate intensity for an average adult—competitive with many other cardio modalities, provided you're not simply gliding on momentum.
- **Upper-body engagement.** Unlike the treadmill or stationary bike, ellipticals with moving handles recruit the upper body, increasing total energy expenditure and offering a full-body workout when used correctly.

**The caveats:**

- The elliptical's fixed stride pattern does not replicate the specificity of running or walking, so it's a complement—not a replacement—for sport-specific training.
- The "coasting" problem is real: many users unconsciously reduce effort by letting the pedals and handles carry them. Resistance and incline settings matter enormously.
- Weight-bearing benefits are lower than with walking or running, which matters for bone health. Pair elliptical work with resistance training and some impact-based activity where appropriate.

**Coaching insight:** "I use the elliptical as a bridge, not a destination," says a certified strength and conditioning specialist with whom we consulted. "It's brilliant for maintaining aerobic fitness during rehab or as a second cardio session that doesn't add pounding. But I always program it with intent—interval structure, resistance targets, and heart-rate zones—otherwise people just spin their wheels, literally."

## How We Tested

Our evaluation combined hands-on use across multiple sessions with a panel of testers spanning beginner, intermediate, and advanced fitness levels. We assessed:

- **Stride length and feel** (typical home models range from 18–20 inches; taller users generally benefit from 20+ inches)
- **Resistance type and range** (magnetic resistance is quieter and smoother than older friction systems)
- **Build stability at speed and incline**
- **Console and connectivity features**
- **Assembly, footprint, and warranty**
- **Value relative to price**

We also weighted **safety features**—such as stable bases, secure foot pedals, and clear emergency-stop mechanisms—given that home cardio equipment injuries are a documented concern. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) has reported thousands of emergency-room visits annually related to exercise equipment, many involving falls and entrapment. Stability and thoughtful design are not luxuries.

## The 6 Best Ellipticals Right Now

### 1. Best Overall: NordicTrack Commercial Series Elliptical
**Best for:** Intermediate to advanced users who want incline variety and immersive training.

NordicTrack's commercial line consistently earns top marks for combining a sturdy build with incline ramps that shift the training stimulus from flat to climbing—a feature that meaningfully changes muscle recruitment and caloric expenditure. The 20-inch stride accommodates taller users, and the magnetic resistance is smooth and quiet. The integrated touchscreen and iFIT programming offer guided sessions, though a subscription is required to unlock the full library.

**Practical tip:** Use the incline feature deliberately. A 2022 study in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* suggested that adding incline to elliptical work increases glute and calf activation and elevates heart rate at the same perceived effort. Start with short 1–2 minute incline intervals rather than long climbs.

**Considerations:** Large footprint; assembly takes time. Budget for the subscription if you want the full experience.

### 2. Best for Beginners: Schwinn 470 Elliptical
**Best for:** New exercisers who want a reliable, affordable machine with guided programs.

The Schwinn 470 hits a sweet spot: a 20-inch stride, 29 resistance levels, and a motorized incline that lets beginners progress gradually. The console offers preset programs and app connectivity (via Schwinn's fitness app) without demanding a premium subscription. It's not the quietest machine at high resistance, but for the price, it's a dependable entry point.

**Beginner protocol:** Start with 15–20 minutes at a comfortable resistance (RPE 3–4 out of 10) three times per week. Add 5 minutes per week until you reach 30–40 minutes. Only then introduce intervals. This gradual progression reduces the risk of overuse irritation in the hips and knees—common when new users jump into long sessions too soon.

**Form cue:** Stand tall, keep a slight bend in the knees, and avoid locking your elbows. Push *and* pull the handles rather than just holding on.

### 3. Best Budget Pick: Sunny Health & Fitness Elliptical
**Best for:** Space- and budget-conscious users who prioritize simplicity.

Sunny's compact models (notably the SF-E3912 and similar) deliver a surprisingly solid workout for a fraction of the cost of premium machines. Stride length is shorter (typically around 15–16 inches), which suits users under about 5'8" better than taller exercisers. Resistance is manual or basic magnetic, and there's no incline—but for steady-state cardio, it gets the job done.

**Coaching insight:** With a budget machine, structure becomes everything. Because you can't rely on incline or immersive programming, use timed intervals: 2 minutes at moderate resistance, 1 minute at high resistance, repeated 8–10 times. This "poor man's interval session" compensates for limited features.

**Safety note:** Check weight capacity carefully (often 220–265 lbs on budget models) and ensure the base is level on your floor. A wobbling machine is an injury waiting to happen.

### 4. Best for Serious Athletes: Precor EFX Series
**Best for:** Advanced users and rehab-focused athletes who want commercial-grade smoothness.

Precor's EFX line is the gold standard in many physical therapy clinics and commercial gyms—and for good reason. The cross-ramp technology and rear-drive design produce an exceptionally natural, joint-friendly motion. The build is rock-solid at speed, and the stride feels fluid even during high-cadence work. It's an investment, but for athletes cross-training around impact injuries or maintaining aerobic capacity during off-seasons, it's hard to beat.

**Advanced protocol:** Use the EFX for "tempo" sessions—20–30 minutes at a heart rate corresponding to 80–85% of max (roughly the top of Zone 3 to low Zone 4). This builds aerobic capacity without the musculoskeletal cost of running tempo work. Pair with one weekly interval session (e.g., 6 × 3 minutes hard / 2 minutes easy).

**Recovery tip:** The elliptical is an excellent "active recovery" tool. Twenty to thirty minutes at low resistance (RPE 3) on an off-day promotes blood flow and can reduce delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) without adding training stress.

### 5. Best Compact Option: Bowflex Max Trainer
**Best for:** Users with limited space who want high-intensity, time-efficient workouts.

The Max Trainer blends elliptical and stepper mechanics into a compact footprint, and its signature 14-minute interval programs are genuinely challenging. Research on high-intensity interval training (HIIT) consistently shows that short, intense sessions can produce aerobic adaptations comparable to longer moderate sessions—making the Max Trainer attractive for busy professionals.

**Reality check on timelines:** HIIT is effective, but it's not a shortcut to instant fitness. Expect 4–6 weeks of consistent 2–3 weekly sessions before you notice meaningful improvements in stamina. Beginners should start with the lower-intensity programs and build up; the machine's intensity can overwhelm new users if they dive into the hardest intervals first.

**Form cue:** Keep your heels down and drive through the whole foot. Avoid bouncing on your toes, which stresses the calves and Achilles.

### 6. Best for Streaming & Entertainment: Peloton-Style Connected Elliptical (e.g., ProForm Carbon EL)
**Best for:** Motivation-dependent exercisers who thrive on classes and community.

Connected fitness has reshaped home cardio. Machines like the ProForm Carbon EL pair with streaming platforms (iFIT, and increasingly third-party apps) to deliver instructor-led sessions, scenic rides, and live metrics. The entertainment value is real: adherence research suggests that enjoyment and variety are strong predictors of exercise consistency.

**Caution:** Don't let the screen do the work. A common trap is choosing classes by entertainment value rather than training stimulus. Alternate "fun" sessions with structured zone-2 and interval days so your program stays balanced.

**Budget alternative:** If a connected machine is out of reach, a basic elliptical plus a tablet and a low-cost app subscription delivers 80% of the experience at a fraction of the price.

## Elliptical Training: A Practical Framework for Every Level

### Beginners (0–3 months)
- **Frequency:** 3 sessions/week, 20–30 minutes
- **Intensity:** RPE 3–5 (able to hold a conversation)
- **Focus:** Consistent form, building the habit, gradual duration increases
- **Expected progress:** Noticeable endurance gains in 3–4 weeks; visible fitness changes typically in 6–8 weeks when paired with nutrition and strength work

### Intermediate (3–12 months)
- **Frequency:** 3–4 sessions/week
- **Structure:** 2 steady-state sessions (30–45 min, Zone 2), 1 interval session (e.g., 8 × 1 min hard / 2 min easy), optional 1 incline or hill session
- **Add:** 2 full-body strength sessions weekly—elliptical work alone won't build the muscle and bone density that support long-term health

### Advanced
- **Frequency:** 4–5 sessions/week as part of a periodized plan
- **Structure:** Mix tempo, threshold intervals, and long steady sessions; use heart-rate or power targets if available
- **Cross-training:** Use the elliptical to add aerobic volume without impact, especially during running build-ups or rehab

**Universal form cues:**
1. Stand tall; avoid hunching over the console.
2. Keep a soft bend in the knees—never lock out.
3. Push and pull the handles with control; don't death-grip.
4. Drive through your whole foot, not just the toes.
5. Keep resistance high enough that you're not freewheeling.

## Nutrition and Recovery: The Missing Pieces

No machine—however excellent—outpaces poor recovery and nutrition.

- **Protein:** Aim for roughly 1.6 g per kg of body weight daily if you're combining cardio with strength training (per position stands from the International Society of Sports Nutrition). Spread intake across meals; a 20–30 g serving post-exercise supports recovery.
- **Hydration:** Even low-impact cardio causes meaningful fluid loss. Drink to thirst and monitor urine color; add electrolytes for sessions over 60 minutes or in hot environments.
- **Sleep:** 7–9 hours is the foundation of adaptation. A 2021 review in *Sports Medicine* linked chronic sleep restriction to impaired endurance performance and elevated injury risk.
- **Deload weeks:** Every 4–6 weeks, reduce volume by 30–40% for a week. This is especially important for home exercisers who tend to either go all-in or stop entirely—neither extreme serves progress.

**Injury-prevention note:** Elliptical use is low-impact but not risk-free. Repetitive fixed-pattern motion can irritate the IT band and hips in some users. Vary stride cadence, incorporate lateral band work and hip mobility drills, and alternate the elliptical with walking, cycling, or swimming to diversify movement patterns.
