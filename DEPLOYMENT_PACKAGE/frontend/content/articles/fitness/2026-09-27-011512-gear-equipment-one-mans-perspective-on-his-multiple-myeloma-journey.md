# Strength Training Through Illness: What One Man's Multiple Myeloma Journey Teaches Us About Resilience, Recovery, and Smart Training

When most of us think about obstacles to our fitness goals, we picture busy schedules, nagging injuries, or a lack of motivation. But for some people, the challenge is far more serious: a cancer diagnosis that reshapes every aspect of life, including how they move, train, and recover.

Multiple myeloma—a cancer of plasma cells in the bone marrow—affects roughly 35,000 Americans each year, according to the American Cancer Society. It's a disease that can weaken bones, sap energy, and complicate even the simplest physical tasks. Yet as one patient's journey illustrates, a diagnosis doesn't mean the end of an active life. With the right approach, movement and structured training can remain powerful tools for physical and emotional resilience.

This article explores what multiple myeloma patients—and anyone facing a serious health challenge—can learn about training safely, eating well, and staying hopeful. We'll also translate those lessons into practical advice for everyday fitness enthusiasts, because resilience, after all, is a skill we all need.

---

## Why Movement Matters More Than Ever During Treatment

Exercise science has increasingly recognized that physical activity is not just safe during cancer treatment—it's often beneficial. A 2022 review in *Frontiers in Oncology* found that supervised exercise programs improved fatigue, quality of life, and physical function in patients with multiple myeloma, with no significant increase in adverse events when programs were appropriately modified.

The key word is *modified*. Multiple myeloma can cause lytic bone lesions (areas of weakened bone), anemia, and peripheral neuropathy. That means the standard "go hard or go home" mentality is not just unhelpful—it can be dangerous. Instead, the goal shifts to maintaining function, preserving muscle mass, and supporting mental health.

**Practical takeaway:** If you're navigating a serious illness, work with a medical team and a qualified trainer or physical therapist to design a program that respects your condition. For healthy lifters, the lesson is equally valuable: train hard, but train smart, and never ignore warning signs from your body.

## Building a Safe Strength Foundation: Beginner to Advanced

Whether you're recovering from illness or simply starting your fitness journey, the principles of safe strength training are remarkably consistent. Here's how to apply them across fitness levels.

### For Beginners

Start with bodyweight movements and light resistance. Focus on form before load.

- **Sit-to-stand from a chair:** 2–3 sets of 8–12 reps. This mimics daily movement and builds foundational leg strength.
- **Wall push-ups or incline push-ups:** 2–3 sets of 8–12 reps. Progress to floor push-ups as tolerated.
- **Resistance band rows:** 2–3 sets of 10–15 reps. Great for posture and upper back strength.

**Form cue:** Keep your spine neutral and core gently braced. Never round your lower back under load, especially if you have bone health concerns.

**Timeline:** Expect noticeable improvements in daily function within 4–6 weeks with consistent practice.

### For Intermediate Lifters

You've mastered the basics. Now you can add moderate loads and more complex movements.

- **Goblet squats:** 3 sets of 8–10 reps.
- **Dumbbell floor press:** 3 sets of 8–10 reps. The floor limits range of motion, which can be safer for shoulders and bones.
- **Seated cable or band rows:** 3 sets of 10–12 reps.

**Recovery tip:** Allow 48 hours between strength sessions for the same muscle groups. Sleep and protein intake are your best recovery tools.

### For Advanced Trainees

If you're healthy and experienced, you can maintain heavier loads—but even then, periodization and deload weeks are essential. A 2023 study in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* reaffirmed that planned deloads reduce injury risk and improve long-term performance.

- **Trap bar deadlifts:** 3–4 sets of 5 reps. The trap bar reduces spinal shear compared to a straight bar.
- **Pull-ups or lat pulldowns:** 3–4 sets of 6–10 reps.
- **Split squats:** 3 sets of 8–10 reps per leg.

**Coaching insight:** Even advanced lifters should include one "joint-friendly" session per week—think sled pushes, carries, or machine-based work—to reduce cumulative stress.

## Nutrition That Supports Recovery and Resilience

You can't out-train a poor diet, and that's especially true when your body is under stress from illness or intense training. Here's what the evidence supports:

- **Protein:** Aim for 1.2–2.0 grams per kilogram of body weight daily, depending on activity level and health status. For a 70 kg (154 lb) person, that's 84–140 grams per day. Spread intake across 3–4 meals.
- **Calcium and vitamin D:** Critical for bone health, especially for myeloma patients and postmenopausal women. Discuss supplementation with your doctor.
- **Anti-inflammatory fats:** Omega-3s from fatty fish, walnuts, and flaxseed may help manage inflammation.
- **Hydration:** Even mild dehydration impairs strength and cognition. Aim for pale-yellow urine as a simple gauge.

**Practical tip:** If appetite is low during treatment or heavy training, prioritize protein and calorie-dense smoothies. Add Greek yogurt, nut butter, and frozen berries for a nutrient-packed shake.

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

Recovery isn't passive—it's an active part of your program.

- **Sleep:** 7–9 hours per night. Poor sleep increases injury risk and slows healing.
- **Stress management:** Chronic stress elevates cortisol, which can impair muscle repair and immune function. Try 10 minutes of diaphragmatic breathing or a short walk outdoors.
- **Listen to your body:** Sharp pain, unusual fatigue, or new numbness are red flags. Stop and consult a professional.
- **Bone health awareness:** If you have or had cancer, avoid high-impact jumping and heavy spinal loading unless cleared by your oncologist.

**Injury prevention cue:** Warm up with 5–10 minutes of light cardio and dynamic stretches. Never stretch cold muscles aggressively.

## Alternatives for Different Equipment and Environments

Not everyone has a gym. Here's how to adapt:

- **No weights?** Use water bottles, backpacks filled with books, or resistance bands.
- **Small space?** Focus on isometrics (wall sits, planks) and bodyweight circuits.
- **Outdoors?** Use park benches for step-ups, dips, and incline push-ups.
- **Traveling?** A single resistance band can replace most dumbbells for upper body work.

**Coaching perspective:** Consistency beats intensity. A 20-minute home workout done three times a week will outperform a sporadic 90-minute gym session.

---

## Hope, Realistic Timelines, and the Long Game

One of the most important lessons from any health journey—whether it's cancer, injury, or simply starting out—is that progress is rarely linear. You'll have good days and bad days. The goal is to keep showing up.

- **Beginner:** Expect functional improvements in 4–6 weeks, visible changes in 8–12 weeks.
- **Intermediate:** Strength gains of 5–10% every 4–6 weeks are realistic with proper programming.
- **Advanced:** Progress slows, but 1–2% improvements per month are still meaningful.

And for those facing serious illness: hope is not naive. Advances in treatment, combined with thoughtful movement and nutrition, are helping more people live longer, stronger lives. As one patient put it, life with a blood cancer is rarely straightforward—but there is a lot of hope for the future.

---

## Final Rep

Whether you're lifting for health, training for performance, or simply trying to stay active through a difficult chapter, the fundamentals remain the same: move intelligently, eat to support your body, recover deliberately, and never underestimate the power of consistency.

Your body is capable of remarkable things. Treat it with respect, and it will carry you further than you think.

**Always consult your physician before starting a new exercise or nutrition program, especially if you have a chronic condition or are undergoing treatment.**
