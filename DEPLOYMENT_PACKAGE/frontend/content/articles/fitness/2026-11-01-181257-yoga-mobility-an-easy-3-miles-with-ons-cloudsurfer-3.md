# An Easy 3 Miles with On's Cloudsurfer 3: A Runner's Honest Review

## Can One City Jog Really Change a Lifelong Brand Loyalist's Mind?

After years of stubborn loyalty to another brand, could one city jog make a longtime runner reconsider his shoes? That question sat with me as I laced up the On Cloudsurfer 3 for a relaxed three-mile loop through the city. What began as a routine shakeout run turned into a small but meaningful lesson about shoe selection, running economy, and the value of staying open-minded—even after years of habit.

If you've ever found yourself buying the same running shoe year after year out of comfort or superstition, this review is for you. We'll break down what the Cloudsurfer 3 offers, how it fits into a broader training plan, and what recent exercise science says about cushioning, cadence, and injury prevention.

---

## First Impressions: What Makes the Cloudsurfer 3 Different

The On Cloudsurfer 3 is a daily trainer built for easy miles and recovery runs. It features On's signature "CloudTec Phase" midsole—a rocker-style geometry designed to guide your foot smoothly from heel strike to toe-off. The shoe weighs in at roughly 8.5–9 ounces (men's US 9), making it a middleweight option suitable for most recreational runners.

**Key specs at a glance:**
- **Best for:** Easy runs, recovery days, daily mileage
- **Drop:** Approximately 10mm
- **Midsole:** CloudTec Phase with a smooth rocker
- **Upper:** Engineered mesh for breathability
- **Outsole:** Rubber pods for road durability

For runners logging 15–30 miles per week (roughly 24–48 km), a shoe like this can serve as a workhorse—especially on the days when your legs need a break from tempo work.

---

## The Run: A Three-Mile Test in the City

My test run was intentionally easy—conversational pace, roughly 10:30 per mile. The goal wasn't speed; it was to assess comfort, transition, and how my legs felt afterward.

**What stood out:**

1. **Smooth heel-to-toe transition.** The rocker geometry did most of the work, reducing the sensation of "slapping" the pavement.
2. **Softer landing.** The cushioning felt forgiving on concrete sidewalks, which is important since pavement can transmit significant impact forces through the lower legs.
3. **Stable enough for easy pace.** While not a stability shoe, the wider base provided confidence on turns.

By mile two, I noticed something surprising: my cadence felt slightly quicker without conscious effort. That's a meaningful observation, because research consistently links higher cadence (roughly 170–180 steps per minute for many runners) with reduced stride length and lower peak loading on the knees and hips.

---

## What Exercise Science Says About Cushioning and Cadence

Recent running research offers useful context for shoe selection:

- **Cushioning doesn't eliminate injury risk—training load does.** A growing body of evidence suggests that sudden spikes in weekly mileage or intensity are stronger predictors of running injury than footwear alone. Most running-related injuries affect the lower leg, with cumulative load being a key factor.
- **Cadence matters.** Studies on step rate manipulation show that increasing cadence by 5–10% can reduce joint loading at the knee and hip. A rocker-style shoe like the Cloudsurfer 3 may naturally encourage a quicker turnover for some runners.
- **Shoe rotation helps.** Sports medicine clinicians increasingly recommend rotating between two or more pairs of shoes to vary loading patterns and reduce repetitive stress.

**Practical takeaway:** No shoe is a magic bullet. The Cloudsurfer 3 is a useful tool—best paired with sensible progression and variety.

## Training Advice: How to Use an Easy-Run Shoe

### For Beginners
- **Start with run/walk intervals.** Try 2 minutes running, 1 minute walking, repeated for 20–25 minutes, three times per week.
- **Keep 80% of runs easy.** You should be able to speak in full sentences.
- **Progress mileage gradually.** A common guideline is no more than a 10% increase in weekly volume.

### For Intermediate Runners
- Use the Cloudsurfer 3 for recovery runs and long easy days.
- Reserve faster shoes for tempo and interval sessions.
- Aim for 20–35 miles (32–56 km) per week, depending on your goals and history.

### For Advanced Runners
- Treat this as a recovery-day shoe. Pair it with a lighter tempo trainer and, if desired, a race-day shoe.
- Monitor your "easy pace" honestly—most athletes run easy days too fast, which blunts recovery.

---

## Nutrition and Recovery: Fueling Easy Miles

Easy runs don't require elaborate fueling, but consistency matters:

- **Hydrate before you head out.** Aim for 400–600ml of water in the 1–2 hours before a short run.
- **Don't skip carbohydrates.** For runs under 60 minutes, no mid-run fuel is necessary, but a balanced meal within a few hours of running supports recovery.
- **Protein after training.** Roughly 20–30g of protein post-run supports muscle repair—think Greek yogurt, eggs, or a shake.
- **Sleep is the real recovery tool.** Most adults need 7–9 hours; athletes often benefit from the higher end.

---

## Safety and Form Cues

Proper form reduces injury risk regardless of shoe choice:

- **Run tall.** Imagine a string pulling the crown of your head upward.
- **Land under your hips.** Avoid overstriding, which increases braking forces.
- **Relax your shoulders and hands.** Tension wastes energy.
- **Watch for warning signs.** Persistent pain, especially on one side, warrants rest and possibly a physiotherapy consult.

**Safety note:** If you're returning from injury or new to running, consult a healthcare professional before increasing mileage significantly.

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

- **Take at least one full rest day per week.**
- **Incorporate mobility work.** Ten minutes of hip and ankle mobility two to three times weekly can improve stride mechanics.
- **Strength train twice weekly.** Focus on glutes, calves, and core—key stabilizers for runners.
- **Use the 48-hour rule.** Avoid back-to-back hard sessions; alternate easy and hard days.

---

## Alternatives for Different Environments and Equipment

- **Treadmill:** The Cloudsurfer 3 works well indoors; the rocker geometry feels smooth on belts.
- **Trail running:** Consider a dedicated trail shoe with more aggressive lugs for off-road miles.
- **Budget option:** Look for previous model years, which often deliver similar performance at lower cost.
- **Minimalist preference:** Some runners prefer lower-drop, firmer shoes—rotate them in gradually rather than switching cold turkey.

---

## Realistic Timelines for Results

- **2–3 weeks:** Improved comfort and rhythm on easy runs.
- **4–6 weeks:** Noticeable aerobic fitness gains if training consistently.
- **8–12 weeks:** Meaningful improvements in endurance and recovery capacity.
- **Shoe lifespan:** Most daily trainers last 300–500 miles (480–800 km), depending on body weight, surface, and running style.

---

## Coaching Perspective: Practical Tips

From a coaching standpoint, the biggest mistake runners make is overcomplicating shoe selection. The Cloudsurfer 3 is a solid, comfortable daily trainer—but it's one piece of a larger puzzle. Focus on consistent, progressive training, honest easy paces, and smart recovery. And every so often, let curiosity win: trying a new shoe, surface, or route can refresh your motivation and reveal something about your running you didn't know.

---

## Final Verdict

Would one city jog make a longtime loyalist switch brands? Not overnight—but it made a convincing case. The On Cloudsurfer 3 delivers a smooth, cushioned ride ideal for easy miles and recovery days. Pair it with sensible training, good nutrition, and consistent recovery, and it can serve runners across the fitness spectrum. Sometimes the best upgrade isn't a new shoe at all—it's an open mind and a well-structured plan.
