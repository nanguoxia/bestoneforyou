# How the Spoto Press Can Help You Break Through Your Bench Press Plateau

If your bench press has been stuck at the same weight for months, you're not alone. Plateaus are one of the most common frustrations in strength training—and the bench press, perhaps more than any other lift, tends to punish lifters who simply add more weight to the bar without addressing technique or weak points.

Enter the Spoto press: a paused bench press variation that forces you to own the most difficult part of the movement. Named after powerlifter Eric Spoto, who at one point held the all-time raw bench press world record, this variation has become a staple in the programs of strength coaches and powerlifters alike. Whether you're a beginner trying to build a solid foundation or an advanced lifter chasing a new personal record, the Spoto press can help you press more weight—safely and strategically.

## What Exactly Is the Spoto Press?

The Spoto press is a bench press variation where you pause the barbell one to three inches above your chest instead of touching down. You hold that position for a brief moment—typically one to three seconds—before pressing the bar back up to lockout.

The key distinction is that the bar never makes contact with your torso. This seemingly small change has significant consequences:

- **It eliminates the "bounce"** that many lifters unconsciously use to muscle the bar off their chest.
- **It increases time under tension** in the most mechanically disadvantaged position of the lift.
- **It demands control and stability** from your shoulders, triceps, and upper back.

By removing the momentum of a chest touch, the Spoto press exposes weaknesses in your pressing mechanics that a standard bench press might mask.

## Why It Works: The Science Behind the Pause

Research on resistance training consistently shows that manipulating tempo and range of motion can drive strength adaptations. A 2021 review published in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* found that paused repetitions at specific joint angles can improve force production at those angles—a concept known as angle-specific strength.

For the bench press, the bottom portion of the lift (where the bar is closest to your chest) is typically the "sticking point" for most lifters. The Spoto press trains exactly that region. Because you're not touching your chest, you can't rely on the stretch-shortening cycle or elastic rebound from your muscles and connective tissue. Instead, you must generate force purely through muscular contraction.

Additional benefits include:

- **Improved bar path control**, since the pause forces you to stabilize the bar in space.
- **Greater triceps engagement**, as the lockout phase becomes more demanding after a dead-stop pause.
- **Enhanced shoulder stability**, because the isometric hold challenges the stabilizer muscles around the shoulder joint.

Anecdotally, coaches report that lifters who incorporate Spoto presses for four to six weeks often see their competition-style bench press jump by 5 to 15 pounds—though individual results vary based on training age, program design, and recovery.

## How to Perform the Spoto Press with Proper Form

Before adding weight, master the movement pattern. Here's a step-by-step breakdown:

1. **Set up on the bench** with your eyes directly under the bar. Grip the bar slightly wider than shoulder width, or use your standard bench press grip.
2. **Retract your shoulder blades** and create a stable arch in your upper back. Your feet should be planted firmly on the floor.
3. **Unrack the bar** and position it over your lower chest, not your neck.
4. **Lower the bar under control** until it's one to three inches above your chest. Think of hovering just above a piece of paper lying on your torso.
5. **Pause for one to three seconds.** Keep your muscles tense—don't relax at the bottom. Your elbows should be at roughly a 45-degree angle to your torso.
6. **Press the bar back up** to lockout without letting it drift toward your face.
7. **Repeat for the prescribed reps**, maintaining the same pause duration each time.

### Key Form Cues
- **"Bend the bar"**: Imagine trying to snap the barbell in half. This engages your lats and stabilizes your shoulders.
- **"Push yourself into the bench"**: Drive your upper back into the pad as you press.
- **"Stay tight everywhere"**: Your glutes, core, and legs should all be engaged, even though this is an upper-body lift.

### Safety Considerations
- Always use a spotter or safety pins when working near your max.
- Start with a weight that's 60–70% of your standard bench press 1RM. The pause makes the lift significantly harder than it looks.
- If you feel sharp pain in your shoulders or elbows, stop and reassess your form or consult a qualified coach.

## Programming the Spoto Press for Different Fitness Levels

The beauty of the Spoto press is its scalability. Here's how to integrate it based on your experience:

### Beginners (0–1 year of training)
- **Frequency**: Once per week, as a secondary bench variation.
- **Sets/Reps**: 3 sets of 6–8 reps with a 1-second pause.
- **Load**: 50–60% of your estimated 1RM.
- **Focus**: Learning the pause and maintaining tension. Don't chase heavy weights yet.

### Intermediate (1–3 years)
- **Frequency**: Once or twice per week, alternating with competition-style bench presses.
- **Sets/Reps**: 4 sets of 4–6 reps with a 2-second pause.
- **Load**: 65–75% of your 1RM.
- **Focus**: Building strength at the sticking point and reinforcing bar path.

### Advanced (3+ years)
- **Frequency**: Twice per week, often as a main lift on one day and a lighter variation on another.
- **Sets/Reps**: 5 sets of 3–5 reps with a 2–3-second pause, or work up to a heavy single with a 1-second pause.
- **Load**: 75–85% of your 1RM for volume work; up to 90% for heavy singles.
- **Focus**: Overcoming specific weak points and peaking for competition.

A practical approach: run a 4-week block where you replace your standard bench press with the Spoto press on one training day, then return to standard benching while keeping the Spoto as an accessory. Many lifters find this "contrast" method effective for breaking plateaus.

## Nutrition and Recovery: The Often-Overlooked Half of the Equation

You can't out-train poor recovery. To get the most out of the Spoto press—or any strength variation—you need to support your body's repair processes.

### Protein Intake
Research suggests that strength athletes benefit from 1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 180-pound (82 kg) lifter, that's roughly 130–180 grams of protein daily. Spread it across three to five meals for optimal muscle protein synthesis.

### Sleep
A 2023 study in *Sports Medicine* reinforced what coaches have long known: sleep deprivation impairs strength performance and recovery. Aim for seven to nine hours of quality sleep per night. If you're training hard, prioritize sleep as much as your training sessions.

### Hydration and Micronutrients
Dehydration can reduce strength output by up to 10%. Drink water throughout the day, and consider electrolytes if you sweat heavily. Ensure adequate intake of magnesium, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids—all linked to muscle function and recovery.

### Deloading
Every four to eight weeks, reduce your training volume by 40–50% for one week. This allows connective tissue and the nervous system to recover, reducing injury risk and setting you up for further progress.

## Practical Coaching Tips from the Trenches

After years of coaching lifters through bench plateaus, a few patterns emerge:

- **Film your sets.** Most lifters think they're pausing an inch above their chest when they're actually touching down. A side-angle video doesn't lie.
- **Don't ego-lift.** The Spoto press will humble you. That's the point. Leave your ego at the door and focus on quality reps.
- **Pair it with rowing.** A strong upper back is a strong bench press. Incorporate barbell rows, dumbbell rows, or face pulls to balance your pressing.
- **Use a tempo trainer.** If you struggle to hold the pause, set a metronome or use a phone timer to enforce the duration.
- **Rotate your variations.** The Spoto press is a tool, not a religion. Cycle it in for 4–8 weeks, then move to another variation like the close-grip bench or floor press.

## Alternatives for Different Equipment and Environments

No barbell? No problem. The Spoto press concept—pausing just above the chest—translates to other tools:

- **Dumbbell Spoto press**: Pause with the dumbbells an inch above your chest. This also increases stabilization demands.
- **Smith machine Spoto press**: Useful for beginners or those training alone, as the fixed bar path reduces the need for a spotter.
- **Resistance band Spoto press**: Anchor bands behind you and perform the same paused motion. Great for home workouts.
- **Push-up Spoto hold**: Lower into a push-up and hover an inch above the floor for 2–3 seconds before pressing up. A bodyweight-friendly option.

## Realistic Timelines for Results

How long before you see carryover to your bench press? Based on coaching experience and available research:

- **2–3 weeks**: Improved control and confidence at the bottom of the bench press.
- **4–6 weeks**: Measurable strength gains at the sticking point; many lifters add 5–10 pounds to their 1RM.
- **8–12 weeks**: Significant carryover, especially if you've addressed other weak points (triceps, upper back, technique).

Remember: progress isn't linear. Some weeks you'll feel stronger; others you'll feel flat. Trust the process, track your numbers, and adjust as needed.

## Final Thoughts

The Spoto press isn't a magic bullet—no single exercise is. But it's a highly effective tool for addressing the most common weak point in the bench press: the bottom position. By forcing you to pause and control the bar just above your chest, it builds strength, stability, and confidence where you need it most.

Whether you're a beginner learning to press with intent or an advanced lifter grinding toward a new PR, give the Spoto press a dedicated block in your program. Pair it with smart nutrition, adequate recovery, and consistent technique work. Your bench press—and your joints—will thank you.

**A note on safety**: Always consult a qualified coach or medical professional before making significant changes to your training, especially if you have a history of shoulder, elbow, or wrist issues. Train hard, but train smart.
