# The Long Game: What a Hit Show’s Uncertain Future Teaches Us About Building Endurance That Lasts

## When the Next Season Isn't Guaranteed, You Train Like It Is

Fans of Taylor Sheridan's high-octane thriller *Lioness* are holding their breath. Despite strong viewership and a committed cast and crew who seem confident the show will continue, Season 4 is no sure thing just yet. That limbo—waiting for a green light that may or may not come—mirrors something every runner knows intimately: the space between the work you've put in and the result you're hoping for.

Here's the lesson worth stealing from the writers' room. Whether or not a fourth season gets the nod, the people behind the show keep showing up. They train, they prepare, they stay ready. That's exactly the mindset that separates runners who improve year after year from those who flame out after one ambitious training block. You don't train for the season you're guaranteed. You train for the one you want.

Let's translate that into miles, minutes, and meals.

## The Science of Staying Ready

Recent exercise science backs up the "always be building" approach. A 2023 review in *Sports Medicine* found that runners who maintain a consistent aerobic base year-round—rather than swinging between extreme peak seasons and total couch-bound off-seasons—retain significantly more mitochondrial density and capillary function in their working muscles. Translation: your engine stays bigger even when you're not racing.

Meanwhile, data from Strava's annual year-in-sport reports consistently shows that runners who log easy miles at least four days per week are roughly 40% less likely to report a running-related injury than those who cram their training into two or three hard sessions. Consistency, not intensity, is the quiet hero.

This matters because uncertainty is the norm. You don't know if your goal race will happen, if your schedule will hold, or if your body will cooperate. What you *can* control is the daily deposit you make into your fitness account.

## Building Your Base: Advice for Every Level

### Beginners (0–20 miles per week)

Your job is not speed. Your job is showing up.

- **Run/walk intervals:** Start with 1 minute of running, 2 minutes of walking, repeated for 20–25 minutes, three times per week. Add one minute of running per interval every two weeks.
- **The talk test:** You should be able to speak a full sentence while running. If you can't, slow down. Most beginners run too fast on easy days—this is the single biggest cause of early quitting.
- **Timeline:** Expect your first comfortable continuous 20-minute run in 6–10 weeks. That's realistic. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

### Intermediate (20–40 miles per week)

Now you can layer in structure without sacrificing the base.

- **The 80/20 rule:** Roughly 80% of your weekly mileage should be genuinely easy, 20% moderate to hard. A 2022 study in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* confirmed that polarized training improves VO2 max and running economy more effectively than a "moderate everything" approach.
- **One quality session per week:** Alternate between tempo runs (20 minutes at a "comfortably hard" effort) and interval work (e.g., 5 x 3 minutes hard with 2 minutes easy jog recovery).
- **Long run progression:** Add 5–10 minutes to your long run each week, then cut back every fourth week to absorb the gains.

### Advanced (40+ miles per week)

You've earned the right to chase performance—but recovery becomes the limiting factor.

- **Double threshold days:** Borrowed from elite Norwegian training, this means two moderate-quality sessions in a day with strict effort caps (think 5k–10k effort, not all-out). It's potent but demanding; only add it after years of consistent training.
- **Strength work twice weekly:** Heavy, low-rep lifts (squats, deadlifts, step-ups) improve running economy by 2–4% in trained runners, according to meta-analyses. That's free speed.
- **Deload every 3–4 weeks:** Reduce volume by 30–40% while keeping intensity. This is when adaptation actually happens.

## Form Cues That Prevent Breakdown

Regardless of level, a few non-negotiables:

- **Cadence:** Aim for 170–180 steps per minute. Shorter, quicker steps reduce braking forces and knee load. Use a metronome app if you tend to overstride.
- **Posture:** Run "tall"—imagine a string pulling the crown of your head upward. Avoid collapsing at the hips late in runs.
- **Foot strike:** Don't force a forefoot strike. Land under your center of mass, whatever that looks like for your anatomy. Forcing a change too quickly causes Achilles and calf injuries.
- **Arm swing:** Drive elbows back, not across your body. Relaxed hands, like you're holding a potato chip.

## Fueling the Work

Nutrition is where many runners leave performance on the table.

- **Daily protein:** Target 1.6–2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight for recovery and adaptation. A 70 kg runner needs roughly 112–154 grams daily. Spread it across 4–5 meals.
- **Carbohydrates are not the enemy:** For runs over 60 minutes, take in 30–60 grams of carbs per hour. For sessions over 2.5 hours, push toward 90 grams per hour using a mix of glucose and fructose.
- **Hydration:** Weigh yourself before and after long runs. Replace 125–150% of the lost weight in fluids over the next few hours. Add electrolytes if you're a salty sweater (check for white streaks on your clothes).
- **The 3:1 recovery window:** Within 30–60 minutes post-run, aim for a 3:1 carb-to-protein ratio—chocolate milk, a banana with peanut butter, or a recovery shake all work.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

The cast and crew of a hit show don't film every scene back-to-back without breaks. Neither should you.

- **Sleep is training:** Athletes who sleep fewer than 7 hours per night are 1.7 times more likely to sustain an injury, per research in *Sleep*. Non-negotiable.
- **Red flags to respect:** Sharp pain, pain that worsens as you run, or pain that lingers more than 48 hours. Stop and assess. Shin splints that persist beyond two weeks warrant a physio visit—early intervention prevents stress fractures.
- **Strength for resilience:** Calf raises (3 x 15, progressing to single-leg), glute bridges, and single-leg deadlifts are your injury insurance. Two 20-minute sessions weekly is enough.
- **Cross-training alternatives:** No treadmill? Try hill repeats, stair workouts, or pool running. No gym? Bodyweight circuits, resistance bands, and isometric holds build meaningful strength.

## Coaching Wisdom for the Uncertain Season

Here's what experienced coaches tell athletes who are waiting for a race confirmation, a schedule to clear, or a body to cooperate:

**Control the controllables.** You can't green-light your goal. You can green-light today's easy run, tonight's sleep, and tomorrow's protein intake. Stack those wins.

**Trust the boring.** The magic isn't in the workout you brag about. It's in the fourth easy run of the week when nobody's watching.

**Stay ready so you don't have to get ready.** When the opportunity comes—a race entry, a PR attempt, a season renewal—you want to be the person who's already been putting in the work, not the one scrambling to build fitness in six weeks.

## The Bottom Line

Whether *Lioness* gets its fourth season or not, the cast and crew have done their job: they stayed prepared, stayed consistent, and stayed ready for the call. That's the entire game in running and fitness. You don't need certainty to make progress. You need a plan, patience, and the humility to run easy when your ego wants to run hard.

Build the base. Fuel the work. Respect recovery. And when the starting gun fires—whenever that is—you'll be ready.

*Always consult a physician before beginning a new training program, especially if you have cardiovascular risk factors, prior injuries, or are returning from a long layoff. Progress gradually, and prioritize consistency over intensity every single time.*
