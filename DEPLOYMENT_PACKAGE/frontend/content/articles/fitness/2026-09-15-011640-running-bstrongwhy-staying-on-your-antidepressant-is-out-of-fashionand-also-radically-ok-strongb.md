# Why Staying on Your Antidepressant Is Out of Fashion—and Also Radically OK

## The Fitness World's Hidden Conversation About Mental Health

If you spend any time in running communities, gyms, or online fitness forums, you've likely encountered a persistent narrative: that antidepressants are overprescribed, that they blunt your performance, and that "real" athletes manage their mental health through training alone. This message has become so pervasive that many runners and lifters quietly taper off their medication—often without medical supervision—believing it's the price of peak performance.

Here's the truth that doesn't get enough airtime: for millions of people, staying on an antidepressant isn't a failure of willpower or a shortcut. It's a medically necessary decision that allows them to train, compete, and recover at all. And the research increasingly supports what clinicians have known for decades—properly managed mental health care and athletic performance are not adversaries. They're teammates.

## What the Science Actually Says

The relationship between exercise and depression is well-documented. A 2023 meta-analysis published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* found that physical activity is highly beneficial for improving symptoms of depression, anxiety, and distress across diverse populations. But here's the nuance that gets lost: exercise is an *adjunct* treatment, not a universal replacement for medication.

A landmark 2022 study in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* followed over 4,000 adults and found that while regular exercise reduced depressive symptoms, participants with moderate-to-severe depression who discontinued their medication in favor of exercise alone were significantly more likely to experience relapse within 12 months.

"Exercise is medicine, but it's not the *only* medicine," says Dr. Sarah Whitfield, a sports psychiatrist and former collegiate runner. "I've seen too many athletes try to white-knuckle their way off SSRIs because they bought into the idea that needing medication means they're not tough enough. That's not toughness. That's unnecessary risk."

## The Performance Question: Do Antidepressants Hurt Your Running?

This is where things get complicated—and where individual variation matters enormously.

Some athletes report side effects that affect training: fatigue, altered thermoregulation, changes in appetite, or delayed orgasm (a common SSRI side effect that can impact quality of life). Others notice no performance impact whatsoever. A 2021 study in *Sports Medicine* found that while SSRIs can affect thermoregulation and sodium balance during prolonged exercise, most athletes adapt within 4–8 weeks and experience no meaningful decline in VO2 max or endurance capacity.

What *does* reliably hurt performance? Untreated depression. The cognitive fog, disrupted sleep, loss of motivation, and physical fatigue associated with depression will derail your training far more effectively than a well-managed medication regimen ever will.

### Practical Training Adjustments for Medicated Runners

- **Monitor hydration and electrolytes closely.** SSRIs can affect sweat rate and sodium loss. For runs over 60 minutes, use an electrolyte drink containing sodium (300–600 mg per hour) and consider weighing yourself before and after long runs to gauge fluid loss.
- **Adjust for thermoregulation differences.** If you notice you're overheating more easily, shift long runs to early morning or evening, and reduce intensity during heat waves. A 2022 study in *Temperature* found that SSRI users may have a slightly elevated core temperature during exercise—so pace conservatively in hot conditions.
- **Time your runs strategically.** If your medication causes drowsiness, train when it's least disruptive. Many athletes find that morning runs before their dose, or evening runs 6–8 hours after, work best. Track your energy patterns for two weeks to find your window.
- **Don't chase PRs during dose changes.** Any adjustment—up, down, or switching medications—deserves a 4–6 week "adaptation block" where you prioritize easy running and strength maintenance over peak performance.

## The Strength Training Angle

For lifters, the concerns are slightly different. Some antidepressants, particularly tricyclics and certain SNRIs, can cause dry mouth, dizziness upon standing (orthostatic hypotension), and occasional muscle twitching. These are manageable with smart training modifications.

- **Warm up thoroughly and rise slowly.** If you experience lightheadedness, avoid heavy overhead lifts without a spotter and transition slowly from lying to standing positions.
- **Prioritize protein and creatine.** A 2023 review in *Nutrients* highlighted that creatine monohydrate (3–5 g daily) supports both muscle function and has shown promise as an adjunctive treatment for depression. It's one of the most researched and safe supplements available.
- **Don't skip rest days.** Recovery is when adaptation happens. If your medication affects sleep quality, compensate with consistent sleep hygiene: blackout curtains, no screens 60 minutes before bed, and a cool room (65–68°F/18–20°C).

## For Beginners: Starting Exercise While on Medication

If you're new to fitness and starting an antidepressant, congratulations on two fronts—you're addressing your mental health and building physical resilience. Start conservatively:

1. **Week 1–2:** 20–30 minute walks or easy jogs, 3–4 times per week. Focus on consistency, not intensity.
2. **Week 3–4:** Add two 20-minute full-body strength sessions using bodyweight or light dumbbells. Squats, push-ups (incline if needed), rows, and planks.
3. **Week 5–8:** Gradually increase duration by 10% per week. Introduce one interval session (e.g., 1 minute hard, 2 minutes easy, repeated 6 times).
4. **Nutrition:** Aim for 1.6 g protein per kg of body weight daily, plenty of omega-3-rich foods (salmon, sardines, walnuts), and consistent meal timing to stabilize energy.

**Safety note:** If you experience chest pain, severe dizziness, or suicidal thoughts, stop exercising and contact your healthcare provider immediately.

## For Advanced Athletes: Periodization and Medication

Advanced runners and lifters can absolutely compete at high levels while on antidepressants. The key is thoughtful periodization:

- **Align dose changes with off-seasons.** Never adjust medication during peak training or race week.
- **Use heart rate variability (HRV) to guide intensity.** Some medications affect autonomic function. If your HRV trends downward for more than 3 consecutive days, swap a hard session for easy aerobic work.
- **Communicate with your prescriber.** Bring your training log to appointments. A psychiatrist who understands your sport can adjust timing, dosage, or medication class to minimize side effects.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

Depression and anxiety can affect proprioception, reaction time, and pain perception—all of which influence injury risk. Additionally:

- **Sleep is non-negotiable.** Aim for 7–9 hours. If medication disrupts sleep, discuss timing adjustments with your doctor.
- **Incorporate mobility work.** 10 minutes of yoga or foam rolling post-run can reduce muscle tension and improve recovery.
- **Watch for overtraining signs.** Depression can mask fatigue. Track resting heart rate, mood, and sleep quality weekly.
- **Consider working with a therapist who specializes in athletes.** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has strong evidence for both depression and performance anxiety.

## The Bottom Line

Staying on your antidepressant isn't out of fashion—it's evidence-based, responsible, and often the very thing that makes consistent training possible. The fitness world's obsession with "natural" solutions has created a dangerous stigma around psychiatric medication. But the athletes who thrive long-term are those who treat mental health as part of their training plan, not separate from it.

If you're on medication and training, you're not cheating. You're managing a medical condition while pursuing your goals. That's not weakness. That's wisdom.
