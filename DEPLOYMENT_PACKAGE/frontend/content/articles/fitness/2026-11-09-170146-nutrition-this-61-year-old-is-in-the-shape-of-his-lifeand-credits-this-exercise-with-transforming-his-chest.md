# This 61-Year-Old Is in the Shape of His Life—and Credits This Exercise with Transforming His Chest

Ricky Dietrich isn't chasing youth—he's outworking it. At 61, the lifelong lifter says he's in the best shape of his life, and he points to one underappreciated movement as the centerpiece of his chest development: the incline cable pullover.

It's a compelling story, but what does the science say? And more importantly, how can you apply the lessons from Dietrich's training to your own program—whether you're picking up a cable handle for the first time or you've been lifting for decades?

## Why the Incline Cable Pullover Deserves a Second Look

The pullover is one of the oldest exercises in the gym, but it's often dismissed as a "finisher" or relegated to bodybuilding lore. The cable version, performed on an incline bench, changes the equation in several meaningful ways:

- **Constant tension:** Unlike dumbbell pullovers, which offer peak resistance only at the bottom of the range, cables maintain tension throughout the entire arc of motion. Research on resistance profiles consistently shows that constant-tension exercises can improve hypertrophy outcomes when programmed intelligently.
- **Favorable stretch position:** A 2023 review in the *Journal of Sports Sciences* highlighted that training muscles in a lengthened position—like the stretched chest and lats at the top of a pullover—may produce superior hypertrophy compared with shortened-position training. The cable pullover puts you in that stretched position under load, safely.
- **Shoulder-friendly loading:** The incline angle reduces the extreme shoulder extension demand of a flat-bench pullover, making it more accessible for lifters with cranky shoulders—a common concern as we age.

For Dietrich, the movement bridges the gap between chest and back training, delivering a deep stretch across the pecs while engaging the lats and serratus. It's a "hybrid" exercise that fits neatly into an upper-body split without adding excessive joint stress.

## How to Perform It With Proper Form

**Setup:**
1. Set a bench to a 30–45 degree incline and position it facing a cable stack with a straight-bar or rope attachment at the high pulley.
2. Lie back with your head toward the stack, feet planted firmly.
3. Grab the attachment with a shoulder-width grip, arms extended overhead.

**Execution:**
- With a slight bend in the elbows, pull the handle in an arc over your head and toward your hips, keeping your arms relatively straight.
- Feel the stretch across your chest and lats at the top; squeeze your pecs and lats as the bar passes your face.
- Control the return over 2–3 seconds—the eccentric is where much of the growth stimulus lives.

**Form cues from coaching:**
- Keep your ribs down and avoid arching your lower back off the bench.
- Don't let your shoulders shrug toward your ears; think "long arms, stable shoulder blades."
- If you feel pinching in the front of the shoulder, reduce the range of motion or lower the incline.

## Programming It for Your Level

**Beginner:** Start with 2 sets of 10–12 reps using a light weight that lets you own the movement pattern. Focus on the stretch and control, not load. Twice per week is plenty.

**Intermediate:** 3 sets of 10–15 reps, placed after your main pressing movement. Use a moderate weight and take the last set close to technical failure.

**Advanced:** 3–4 sets of 8–12 reps with a 2–3 second eccentric. Consider pairing it as a superset with a pressing movement (e.g., incline dumbbell press) to maximize chest pump and time under tension.

## Nutrition and Recovery: The Other Half of the Equation

Dietrich's results aren't just about one exercise—they reflect decades of consistency. For lifters over 50, two nutrition priorities stand out:

- **Protein intake:** Research suggests older adults benefit from 1.2–1.6 g of protein per kilogram of bodyweight daily to counter age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia). Spread intake across 3–4 meals, with 25–40 g per meal.
- **Caloric alignment:** To build muscle while staying lean, aim for a slight surplus (100–200 kcal) if gaining is the goal, or maintenance with high protein if recomposition is the target.

**Recovery matters more with age.** Prioritize 7–9 hours of sleep, schedule at least one full rest day per week, and consider deloading every 6–8 weeks. Shoulder health is non-negotiable—include rotator cuff and scapular work (face pulls, external rotations) twice weekly.

## Realistic Timelines

- **Weeks 1–4:** Improved mind-muscle connection and stretch tolerance.
- **Weeks 6–10:** Noticeable chest fullness and better posture as lats and serratus engage.
- **Months 3–6:** Visible hypertrophy if training and nutrition are consistent.

Progress isn't linear, and at any age, consistency beats intensity. Track your reps and load, and add small increments only when form stays crisp.

## Equipment Alternatives

No cable stack? Try:
- **Resistance bands** anchored high—band pullovers offer similar tension.
- **Dumbbell pullovers** on a flat or slight incline bench (lighter load, controlled tempo).
- **Machine pullover** if your gym has one—great for beginners learning the pattern.

## The Bottom Line

Ricky Dietrich's story is a reminder that smart exercise selection, progressive overload, and recovery can keep you strong well into your 60s—and beyond. The incline cable pullover isn't magic, but it's a smart, joint-friendly tool that deserves a place in your program. Pair it with adequate protein, quality sleep, and patient progression, and you'll give your chest—and your whole body—a genuine chance to transform.

*Always consult a qualified professional before starting a new exercise program, especially if you have existing shoulder or cardiovascular conditions.*
