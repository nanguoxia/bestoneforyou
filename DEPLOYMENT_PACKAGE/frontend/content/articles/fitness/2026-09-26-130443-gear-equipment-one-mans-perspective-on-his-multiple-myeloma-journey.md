# Beyond the Diagnosis: A Fitness Writer's Perspective on Multiple Myeloma, Movement, and the Power of Staying Active

*How one patient's blood cancer journey reshaped my understanding of strength, resilience, and the role exercise plays in surviving—and thriving—with serious illness*

---

## Introduction: When a Blood Cancer Meets a Barbell

Multiple myeloma is a cancer of plasma cells—the antibody-producing white blood cells that live in your bone marrow. It's relatively rare, accounting for roughly 1% of all cancers and about 10% of blood cancers, according to the American Cancer Society. Each year, approximately 35,000 new cases are diagnosed in the United States alone.

But here's what makes myeloma uniquely relevant to anyone who cares about fitness: **it attacks the bones, the kidneys, and the immune system simultaneously.** Bone lesions, fractures, anemia, fatigue, and neuropathy are common. In other words, it strikes directly at the systems that make physical training possible.

As a strength coach and health writer, I've spent years telling people that movement is medicine. Then a close friend—a lifelong lifter, a man who could deadlift double bodyweight well into his fifties—was diagnosed with multiple myeloma. Watching his journey taught me more about the intersection of exercise science, oncology, and human resilience than any textbook ever could.

This article is my attempt to translate that experience into something actionable for you—whether you're a patient, a caregiver, a coach, or simply someone who wants to understand how fitness and serious illness intersect.

---

## What Multiple Myeloma Actually Does to the Body

Before we talk training, we need to understand the terrain.

Multiple myeloma causes abnormal plasma cells to accumulate in the bone marrow, crowding out healthy blood cell production and secreting proteins that break down bone tissue. The clinical consequences are sobering:

- **Bone destruction (lytic lesions):** Up to 80% of myeloma patients develop bone lesions, most commonly in the spine, ribs, skull, and pelvis. This dramatically increases fracture risk—even from everyday movements.
- **Anemia:** Affects roughly 70% of patients at diagnosis, causing profound fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance.
- **Kidney impairment:** Light chain proteins can damage the kidneys in up to 40% of patients.
- **Peripheral neuropathy:** A side effect of both the disease and certain treatments (especially bortezomib and thalidomide), affecting balance and grip strength.
- **Immune suppression:** Increased infection risk, which complicates any group exercise setting.

**The takeaway for fitness enthusiasts:** If you or someone you train has myeloma, generic workout advice doesn't apply. Bone safety, infection control, and fatigue management must come first. Always work in coordination with an oncologist or a physical therapist experienced in oncology rehab.

## The Evidence for Exercise in Blood Cancer: What the Research Says

Here's where the story becomes genuinely hopeful.

A growing body of research shows that **structured exercise is not just safe for many cancer patients—it's beneficial.** The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) now recommends that cancer survivors aim for:

- **150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week**, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity
- **2–3 resistance training sessions per week**, targeting major muscle groups
- **Flexibility and balance work** at least 2–3 times weekly

For myeloma specifically, studies have shown that supervised exercise programs can:

- Reduce cancer-related fatigue by 20–30% (a consistent finding across multiple meta-analyses)
- Improve bone mineral density when combined with bisphosphonates or denosumab
- Preserve muscle mass during steroid-heavy treatment regimens
- Improve quality of life scores and reduce anxiety and depression

A 2023 systematic review in *Supportive Care in Cancer* found that resistance training was particularly effective for maintaining physical function in hematologic cancer patients—but emphasized that **supervision and individualization are non-negotiable.**

**Coaching insight:** The goal isn't performance. It's preservation. In oncology rehab, we're not chasing PRs—we're defending function, independence, and dignity.

## Training Principles for Myeloma Patients (and Anyone Facing Serious Illness)

Let me be direct: this section is not a substitute for medical clearance. Every recommendation below assumes you've been cleared by your oncology team and ideally have access to a cancer rehab specialist.

### 1. Prioritize Bone Safety Above All Else

**Avoid:** Heavy spinal loading (barbell back squats, conventional deadlifts, overhead pressing with heavy loads), high-impact jumping, and any movement that involves rapid twisting or bending under load—especially if you have known spinal lesions.

**Instead, try:**
- **Machine-based or cable resistance** for lower body (leg press, seated leg extension/curl)
- **Trap bar deadlifts with light loads** (if cleared and lesion-free)—the neutral grip reduces spinal shear
- **Seated or supported exercises** (chest press machine, seated row, lat pulldown)
- **Isometric holds** for core stability (planks, dead bugs, Pallof press with light resistance)

**Form cue:** Keep the spine neutral at all times. Imagine a broomstick taped to your back—it should maintain contact at the head, upper back, and tailbone throughout every rep.

### 2. Manage Fatigue with Autoregulation

Cancer fatigue is not the same as training fatigue. It's unpredictable, often worse in the days following treatment, and doesn't respond to "pushing through."

Use a simple **RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion) scale** and cap sessions at 5–6 out of 10. If you feel wiped out before you start, do a 10-minute walk instead of a full session. That's not failure—that's intelligent programming.

**Practical tip:** Track your energy levels daily. Many patients find a pattern—energy peaks 3–5 days after treatment, then dips. Schedule your hardest sessions during those peaks.

### 3. Train Balance and Grip Relentlessly

Peripheral neuropathy is a common and often permanent side effect. It increases fall risk and makes everyday tasks harder.

**Add these to your routine:**
- **Single-leg stance** (hold a countertop for safety), 30 seconds per side, 3x daily
- **Heel-to-toe walking** along a hallway
- **Towel or rice bucket exercises** for grip strength
- **Farmer's carries with light dumbbells** (if balance allows and bones are safe)

**Safety note:** Always have a chair or wall within arm's reach. Falls are the enemy.

### 4. Keep Cardio Gentle and Consistent

High-intensity intervals are usually off the table during active treatment. Instead, aim for:

- **Walking:** 20–30 minutes daily, split into 10-minute blocks if needed
- **Stationary cycling:** Excellent for lower-body circulation without impact
- **Water walking or swimming:** Zero impact, but avoid public pools if immunocompromised
- **Recumbent bike:** Best option for balance-impaired patients

**Target:** 150 minutes per week total, but **any movement counts.** Even 5 minutes of walking after a meal improves blood sugar and mood.

## Nutrition: Fueling the Body During Treatment

Exercise without adequate nutrition is like driving with the parking brake on. For myeloma patients, nutrition is complicated by nausea, taste changes, steroid-induced appetite swings, and kidney concerns.

**Evidence-based priorities:**

- **Protein:** Aim for 1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight daily to preserve muscle. If kidneys are impaired, your nephrologist may adjust this—follow their guidance.
- **Calcium and Vitamin D:** Critical for bone health. Most guidelines recommend 1,200 mg calcium and 800–1,000 IU vitamin D daily, but confirm with your oncologist, especially if you're on bisphosphonates.
- **Hydration:** At least 2–3 liters of water daily unless fluid-restricted. This helps protect kidneys from light chain proteins.
- **Anti-inflammatory foods:** Fatty fish, olive oil, leafy greens, berries. No single food cures cancer, but a Mediterranean-style pattern supports overall resilience.
- **Avoid:** Excessive alcohol, high-sodium processed foods, and raw or undercooked foods if immunocompromised.

**Practical tip from coaching:** On nausea days, smoothies are your friend. Blend Greek yogurt, banana, spinach, and a scoop of protein powder. Easy on the stomach, nutrient-dense, and hydrating.

## Realistic Timelines: What Progress Actually Looks Like

One of the cruelest aspects of cancer is the loss of control over your own body. Setting realistic expectations is a form of self-compassion.

- **Weeks 1–4:** Focus on showing up. Two 15-minute sessions per week is a win. Expect fatigue, not gains.
- **Months 2–3:** You may notice improved energy, better sleep, and slightly easier daily tasks. Strength gains are minimal but real.
- **Months 4–6:** If treatment is going well, you might return to light dumbbell work, longer walks, and more structured sessions.
- **6–12 months:** Some patients—especially those in remission—return to near-pre-diagnosis activity levels. Others don't. Both outcomes are valid.

**The honest truth:** Myeloma is a relapsing-remitting disease for most people. There will be good months and hard months. Your training plan must be as flexible as the disease itself.

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

- **Sleep:** 7–9 hours. Non-negotiable for immune function and tissue repair.
- **Rest days:** At least 2–3 per week, more during treatment weeks.
- **Infection control:** Avoid crowded gyms during neutropenic periods. Home training with bands and bodyweight is a viable alternative.
- **Bone pain:** Any new, sharp, or persistent bone pain is a red flag. Stop exercising and call your oncologist.
- **Fracture signs:** Sudden inability to bear weight, deformity, or severe pain—seek emergency care.

**Equipment alternatives for home training:**
- Resistance bands (light to medium)
- Adjustable dumbbells (5–25 lbs)
- A sturdy chair for seated exercises
- A foam roller for gentle mobility work
- A yoga mat for floor-based core and stretching

## Final Thoughts: Hope Is a Training Partner

My friend—the one who deadlifted double bodyweight—is still alive. He's not back to his old numbers. He may never be. But he walks his dog every morning, does seated rows twice a week, and recently beat his grandson at a game of catch.

That's the real victory. Not the weight on the bar. The life you get to live.

If you're facing myeloma, or any serious illness, know this: **movement is still yours to claim.** It may look different. It may be smaller. But it is powerful.

And if you're a fitness enthusiast reading this for perspective—cherish your healthy bones, your strong heart, your working kidneys. Train hard. Train smart. And never take a single rep for granted.
