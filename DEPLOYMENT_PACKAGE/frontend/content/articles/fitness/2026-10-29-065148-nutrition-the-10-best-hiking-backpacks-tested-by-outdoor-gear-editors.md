# Fueling the Trail: A Fitness Writer's Guide to Nutrition and Training for Hikers (and How to Choose the Right Pack)

*Note: The original piece was a gear roundup. Rather than rewrite a shopping list, this article reframes the topic around what actually determines hiking performance—nutrition, training, and recovery—while still offering practical guidance on choosing and using a pack. Gear matters, but it's the fuel and preparation that carry you up the mountain.*

---

## Why Hiking Is a Legitimate Training Modality

Hiking often gets filed under "just walking," but the physiology tells a different story. Loaded hiking—carrying a pack—raises energy expenditure substantially compared to unloaded walking, and uphill gradients can push heart rate into zones that rival moderate-to-vigorous cardio sessions. Research on trekking consistently shows that multi-day hikes place meaningful demands on aerobic capacity, muscular endurance (especially in the quadriceps, glutes, calves, and core), and connective tissue resilience.

In other words: if you're planning a serious hike or backpacking trip, you should train for it and fuel for it the way you would any endurance event.

---

## Part 1: Nutrition for Hiking—Before, During, and After

### The Day Before and Morning Of

Your body runs on glycogen, and glycogen stores are finite. For hikes lasting more than 90 minutes, a carbohydrate-forward meal the evening before (rice, pasta, potatoes, oats) supports liver and muscle glycogen. On the morning of, aim for a familiar, easily digestible breakfast roughly 2–3 hours before you start—think oatmeal with banana, or toast with eggs. If you're hiking within an hour of waking, a smaller, simple carb snack (a banana or a sports drink) is more comfortable than a heavy meal.

**Coaching tip:** Practice your hike-day breakfast on training hikes. Race-day (or trail-day) nutrition should never be a first experiment.

### Fueling on the Trail

The single biggest mistake hikers make is under-eating. General guidance for endurance activity suggests roughly **30–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour** for moderate efforts, scaling toward the higher end for steep, loaded climbs or very long days. For multi-day trips, consistent intake—not heroic single meals—is what prevents the dreaded "bonk."

Practical trail foods:
- **Quick carbs:** energy chews, dried fruit, pretzels, granola bars
- **Sustained energy:** nut butter packets, trail mix, oat-based bars
- **Real food (for longer trips):** tortillas with cheese, instant rice meals, couscous

**Sodium matters.** Sweat losses vary enormously between individuals, but on hot or high-effort days, including salty snacks or an electrolyte mix helps maintain fluid balance and can reduce cramping risk. A reasonable starting point is a few hundred milligrams of sodium per hour, adjusted to conditions and your own sweat profile.

### Hydration

Aim for roughly **400–800 ml of fluid per hour**, adjusted for heat, altitude, and effort. A useful field test: pale-straw urine suggests adequate hydration; dark urine is a signal to drink more. Don't overdrink plain water on very long efforts without electrolytes—hyponatremia is rare but real.

### Post-Hike Recovery

Within a couple of hours of finishing, combine **protein (roughly 20–40 g)** with **carbohydrates** to support muscle repair and glycogen replenishment. Chocolate milk, a recovery shake, or a proper meal with lean protein and rice all work. Protein intake spread across the day—around **1.4–2.0 g per kg of bodyweight** for active individuals—supports tissue repair, which matters especially on multi-day trips.

## Part 2: Training to Carry a Load

### Beginners (0–8 weeks out)

Start with unloaded walking on varied terrain, 3–4 times per week, building to 60–90 minutes. Add two weekly strength sessions focused on the posterior chain and core:

- **Goblet squats** (3×10) — knees tracking over toes, chest tall
- **Romanian deadlifts or hip hinges** (3×8) — neutral spine, push hips back
- **Step-ups** (3×8 per leg) — drive through the whole foot, control the descent
- **Planks and dead bugs** (3×30–45 seconds / 3×10 per side) — brace, don't hold your breath

**Form cue:** On step-ups and descents, slow the lowering phase—eccentric control is your best insurance against knee pain on downhill sections.

### Intermediate (8–12 weeks out)

Introduce **loaded hikes**: start with 5–7 kg and progress gradually (roughly 10% per week). Add one longer hike weekly, building toward 60–70% of your target distance and elevation. Keep strength training twice weekly, adding single-leg work like split squats and calf raises.

### Advanced and Multi-Day Preparation

Simulate your event: back-to-back hikes on consecutive days with your actual pack weight. Prioritize **eccentric quad strength** for descents and **ankle stability** (single-leg balance work, calf raises). Advanced hikers can tolerate more volume, but the principle of progressive overload still applies—more is only better if you recover from it.

**Realistic timeline:** Expect noticeable fitness gains in 4–6 weeks and meaningful hiking-specific conditioning in 8–12 weeks. Multi-day readiness generally takes a full training block plus at least one rehearsal trip.

## Part 3: Recovery and Injury Prevention

- **Sleep is non-negotiable.** Most tissue repair happens during sleep; aim for 7–9 hours, especially around big training days.
- **Downhill running and hiking load the knees and quads heavily.** Build eccentric tolerance gradually and use trekking poles on steep descents to offload the joints.
- **Watch for overuse signals:** persistent shin, knee, or Achilles soreness that worsens across sessions warrants a deload week, not more miles.
- **Mobility, not just stretching:** hip and ankle mobility work supports efficient stride mechanics under load.

## Part 4: Choosing and Using a Pack (Practical Gear Guidance)

Since gear is part of the equation, here's how to think about it objectively rather than by brand hype:

- **Volume:** Day hikes generally need 15–30 L; overnight trips typically 40–60 L; multi-day expeditions 60 L+. Match volume to your actual kit, not ambition.
- **Fit is everything:** Aim for 80–90% of the load on your hips via a well-fitted hip belt; shoulder straps should stabilize, not carry. Try packs loaded in-store and check that the frame length matches your torso.
- **Weight:** Ultralight packs save grams but often sacrifice load transfer and durability—choose based on your load and terrain.
- **Test before you trust:** Do a loaded training hike with any new pack before a big trip. Blisters and hot spots are easier to fix at home.

**Alternatives by environment:** Frameless ultralight packs suit fast-and-light summer trips; framed packs with adjustable suspension suit heavier loads and rough terrain; hydration-vest style packs work well for fast day hikes.

---

## The Bottom Line

The best hiking preparation isn't a single purchase—it's a plan. Fuel consistently (30–60 g carbs/hour on the trail, protein and carbs after), train progressively with load, respect recovery, and choose gear that fits your body and your trip. Whether you're a weekend walker or a multi-day thru-hiker, those fundamentals will carry you farther than any single piece of equipment.

*Always consult a healthcare professional before starting a new training program, especially if you have cardiovascular, joint, or metabolic conditions.*
