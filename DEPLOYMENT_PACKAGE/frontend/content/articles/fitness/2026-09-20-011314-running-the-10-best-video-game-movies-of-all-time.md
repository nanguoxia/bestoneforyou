# The 10 Best Running Workouts of All Time

*And yes—a few worthy cross-training sessions too.*

Ask ten runners to name the single best workout, and you'll get ten different answers. Ask them to name the ten best, and you'll get a lively argument that lasts until the coffee goes cold. That's the beauty of running: the sport rewards experimentation, and the workouts that endure do so because they work across decades, distances, and ability levels.

What follows is my personal canon—ten sessions that have survived the test of time, backed by exercise science and refined by coaches from the high school track to the Olympic marathon. Whether you're chasing your first 5K or your fiftieth marathon, there's something here for you. Let's lace up.

---

## 1. The Easy Run (Yes, Really)

**Why it's elite:** It sounds too simple to make a "best of" list, but the humble easy run is the backbone of every successful training plan ever written. Research consistently shows that roughly 80 percent of elite runners' weekly volume is performed at low intensity—a polarized approach that builds aerobic capacity while managing fatigue.

**How to do it:** Run at a conversational pace. If you can't speak in full sentences, slow down. Heart rate should sit around 65–75 percent of maximum, or roughly Zones 1–2.

**Beginner adjustment:** Start with 20–30 minutes, three times per week. Walk breaks are not cheating; they're pacing tools.

**Advanced application:** Even at 100+ miles per week, the easy run stays easy. Ego is the enemy here.

**Coach's tip:** "If you feel like you could go faster, that's exactly the right effort." The hardest skill in running is running slow enough.

---

## 2. The Tempo Run

**Why it's elite:** Tempo runs—sometimes called "threshold runs"—teach your body to clear lactate efficiently and hold a strong pace without redlining. They're the single most reliable predictor of race performance across distances from 5K to marathon.

**How to do it:** After a 10–15 minute warm-up, run 20–40 minutes at a "comfortably hard" effort—roughly the pace you could sustain for an hour in a race. It should feel controlled, not desperate.

**Beginner adjustment:** Try 3 x 5 minutes at tempo with 2 minutes of easy jogging between.

**Advanced application:** Extend to 2 x 20 minutes or a continuous 40–50 minute block during marathon builds.

**Safety note:** Proper form matters here—stay tall, relax your shoulders, and avoid overstriding as fatigue builds. If your cadence drops sharply, you've gone too hard.

---

## 3. The Long Run

**Why it's elite:** The long run builds mitochondrial density, fat oxidation, and mental toughness in one package. For marathoners, it's non-negotiable. For everyone else, it's the session that makes everything else possible.

**How to do it:** Keep the pace easy—most coaches recommend 60–90 seconds per mile slower than goal race pace. Duration matters more than speed.

**Beginner adjustment:** Build gradually. Add no more than 10 percent to your longest run each week, and take a down week every third or fourth week.

**Advanced application:** Add a "fast finish"—the last 20–30 minutes at marathon or tempo pace—to simulate late-race fatigue.

**Recovery consideration:** The day after a long run should be genuinely easy or off. This is where adaptation happens.

---

## 4. Hill Repeats

**Why it's elite:** Hills are strength training in disguise. They build power, improve running economy, and reduce injury risk because the incline naturally shortens your stride and lowers impact forces compared to flat sprinting.

**How to do it:** Find a hill with a 4–8 percent grade. Warm up thoroughly, then run 6–10 repeats of 30–60 seconds at a hard but controlled effort. Jog or walk down for recovery.

**Beginner adjustment:** Start with 4–6 repeats of 20–30 seconds at a moderate effort. Focus on quick, light steps.

**Advanced application:** Progress to longer repeats (90 seconds to 2 minutes) or steeper grades. Some elites use hill circuits for 8–12 x 2 minutes.

**Form cue:** Drive your arms, lean slightly from the ankles (not the waist), and keep your feet landing under your hips.

**No hills nearby?** Treadmill inclines, stadium stairs, or parking garage ramps all work.

---

## 5. The Fartlek

**Why it's elite:** Swedish for "speed play," the fartlek is unstructured interval training—and it's one of the most versatile sessions in existence. It builds speed, endurance, and the ability to surge in races, all without the pressure of a track.

**How to do it:** After warming up, alternate faster segments with easy recovery based on feel. A classic version: 1 minute hard, 2 minutes easy, repeated 8–10 times. Or use landmarks—sprint to the next lamppost, jog to the next tree.

**Beginner adjustment:** Keep the "hard" segments at a controlled 5K-to-10K effort and shorten them to 30–45 seconds.

**Advanced application:** Use fartlek during base season to introduce intensity without formal structure. It's also a great way to break up a long run.

**Coach's tip:** Fartlek teaches pacing intuition better than any GPS watch. Try it without looking at your pace—just run by feel.

---

## 6. Track Intervals (VO2 Max Work)

**Why it's elite:** Short, fast repeats at 3K–5K effort raise your VO2 max—the maximum rate at which your body can use oxygen—which is one of the strongest predictors of endurance performance. These sessions are sharp, effective, and humbling.

**How to do it:** After a thorough warm-up and a few strides, run repeats such as 6–10 x 400m, 5 x 800m, or 4 x 1000m at 5K pace, with 60–90 percent recovery jogs.

**Beginner adjustment:** Start with 4–6 x 200m at a controlled fast pace, walking or jogging 200m between.

**Advanced application:** Progress to 12 x 400m or 6 x 1000m during 5K–10K training blocks.

**Safety note:** Speed work demands a proper warm-up—at least 15 minutes of easy running plus dynamic drills and strides. Never sprint cold.

**No track access?** Use a measured stretch of road, a flat trail, or even a treadmill with precise pace control.

---

## 7. The Progression Run

**Why it's elite:** A progression run starts easy and finishes fast, teaching you to negative-split—run the second half of a race faster than the first. It's a favorite of marathon coaches because it mimics how smart racers actually run.

**How to do it:** Begin at an easy pace, then gradually increase speed every 10–15 minutes. Finish the last 10–20 minutes at tempo or slightly faster.

**Beginner adjustment:** Progress across 30 total minutes: 10 easy, 10 moderate, 10 comfortably hard.

**Advanced application:** Run 60–90 minutes with the final third at marathon pace or faster.

**Recovery consideration:** Because the finish is hard, treat the day after as easy. Don't stack progression runs back-to-back.

---

## 8. Strides

**Why it's elite:** Strides are 15–20 second accelerations to about 90 percent of sprint speed, followed by full recovery. They improve neuromuscular coordination, running economy, and leg turnover—and they're gentle enough to do several times per week.

**How to do it:** After an easy run, find a flat, smooth surface. Accelerate smoothly to fast-but-relaxed for 15–20 seconds, then decelerate gradually. Walk or jog back. Repeat 4–6 times.

**Beginner adjustment:** Start with 4 strides at 70–80 percent effort. Focus on relaxation and quick feet, not maximum speed.

**Advanced application:** Incorporate strides into warm-ups before workouts or races. Many elites do 6–8 before a hard session.

**Form cue:** Stay tall, keep your shoulders loose, and avoid clenching your fists. Think "fast and smooth," not "fast and tense."

---

## 9. The Recovery Run

**Why it's elite:** The recovery run is the unsung hero of high-volume training. It increases blood flow to tired muscles, promotes nutrient delivery, and helps clear metabolic byproducts—all while adding aerobic volume at minimal cost.

**How to do it:** Run very easy—slower than your normal easy pace. Heart rate should stay in Zone 1. If you're breathing hard, you're doing it wrong.

**Beginner adjustment:** If a recovery run leaves you more tired, replace it with a walk, cycle, or swim. Cross-training counts.

**Advanced application:** Keep recovery runs strictly easy, even during heavy training blocks. The temptation to push is real; resist it.

**Coach's tip:** "The recovery run should feel almost embarrassingly slow." That's the point.

---

## 10. Cross-Training (The Worthy Guest Star)

**Why it's elite:** Running is a high-impact sport, and sometimes the best way to improve is to do something else. Cycling, swimming, rowing, and elliptical training build aerobic fitness with zero impact, making them invaluable during injury rehab or as a supplement to running.

**How to do it:** Match the duration and intensity of the run you're replacing. A 60-minute easy run becomes a 60-minute easy bike ride. Tempo work translates well to the bike or elliptical.

**Beginner adjustment:** If you're new to running, cross-training can build your aerobic base while your joints adapt to impact.

**Advanced application:** Many elites use cross-training to add volume without adding injury risk. It's not a replacement for running specificity, but it's a powerful supplement.

**Safety note:** If you're cross-training through an injury, get cleared by a physio or physician first. And remember: cycling fitness doesn't fully transfer to running fitness—specificity matters.

## Putting It All Together

No single workout makes a runner. The magic is in the mix: mostly easy running, a couple of quality sessions per week, a long run, and enough recovery to absorb the work. A classic week might look like this:

- **Monday:** Easy run + strides
- **Tuesday:** Tempo or intervals
- **Wednesday:** Easy run or cross-training
- **Thursday:** Hill repeats or fartlek
- **Friday:** Rest or recovery run
- **Saturday:** Long run
- **Sunday:** Easy run or rest

Adjust the volume and intensity to your experience, goals, and life. Beginners might run three days per week with two cross-training days. Advanced runners might double on easy days and add a second quality session.

## Nutrition and Recovery Essentials

- **Fuel before quality sessions:** Eat a light carb-based snack 60–90 minutes before hard workouts. Think banana, toast, or a small bowl of oatmeal.
- **Hydrate consistently:** Even 2 percent dehydration can impair performance. Aim for pale-yellow urine as a simple gauge.
- **Protein for repair:** Aim for 1.6–2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily, spread across meals.
- **Sleep is training:** Most adaptation happens during sleep. Seven to nine hours is the target for most runners.
- **Listen to your body:** Persistent fatigue, rising resting heart rate, and nagging aches are early warning signs. Back off before they become injuries.

## Realistic Timelines

- **Beginner:** Expect noticeable fitness gains in 4–6 weeks with consistent training. A first 5K is realistic in 8–12 weeks.
- **Intermediate:** Improvements in race times typically appear over 8–16 week training blocks.
- **Advanced:** Marginal gains take longer—think 12–20 weeks per peak. Patience is a performance enhancer.

## The Bottom Line

The best workout is the one you'll actually do—consistently, intelligently, and with enough recovery to keep showing up. These ten sessions have earned their place because they work for runners of all levels, from first-timers to Olympians. Mix them thoughtfully, respect the easy days, and give your body the fuel and rest it needs.

Now go run. The workouts are waiting.
