# Strength Training with Crohn's Disease: How One Man Built Muscle and Health Through Fitness

## A Story of Resilience

When Craig Collins first faced the challenges of Crohn's disease, the idea of becoming strong seemed impossible. The chronic inflammatory bowel condition brought fatigue, unpredictable flare-ups, and a body that seemed to work against him. Yet through multiple surgeries and a committed fitness journey, Collins transformed his health in ways he once thought unattainable.

His story raises an important question for the millions living with chronic conditions: **Can you actually build strength and improve your health when your body presents unique challenges?** The answer, according to both research and real-world experience, is a qualified yes—with the right approach.

---

## Understanding Crohn's Disease and Exercise

Crohn's disease affects an estimated **1.6 million Americans** and millions more worldwide, according to the Crohn's & Colitis Foundation. It's a chronic inflammatory condition of the digestive tract that can cause abdominal pain, fatigue, malnutrition, and unpredictable symptom flares.

### What the Science Says About Exercise and IBD

Recent research offers encouraging findings. A **2022 systematic review** published in *Therapeutic Advances in Gastroenterology* found that moderate exercise is generally safe for people with inflammatory bowel disease and may reduce fatigue, improve quality of life, and support mental health. Some studies even suggest that regular physical activity may help reduce the risk of flare-ups, though more research is needed.

**However**, the key word is *moderate*—and individualized. Exercise during active flares requires caution, and anyone with Crohn's should work closely with their gastroenterologist before starting or modifying a training program.

## Building Strength: A Realistic Framework

Collins's journey wasn't about heroic workouts—it was about consistency, patience, and adaptation. Here's how to apply those principles, regardless of your starting point.

### For Beginners: Start Smaller Than You Think

If you're new to strength training or returning after illness, the biggest mistake is doing too much too soon.

**Practical starting points:**
- Begin with **2 full-body sessions per week**, 20–30 minutes each
- Focus on bodyweight movements: chair squats, wall push-ups, glute bridges, and light resistance band work
- Use the **RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) scale**: aim for a 5–6 out of 10—challenging but sustainable
- Prioritize **form over load**. A well-executed bodyweight squat builds more than a sloppy weighted one

**Form cue to remember:** For squats, keep your chest tall, knees tracking over toes, and weight through your mid-foot. If balance is an issue, hold a doorframe or sturdy chair.

### For Intermediate Lifters: Build Progressive Overload Carefully

Once you've established a routine without triggering symptoms, you can begin adding load systematically.

- Incorporate **compound movements**: goblet squats, dumbbell rows, presses, and hip hinges
- Progress by **small increments**—2.5–5% increases in weight or one to two additional reps per week
- Track your sessions in a simple log; patterns (good and bad) become visible over 4–6 weeks

### For Advanced Trainees: Manage Fatigue and Flares

Experienced lifters with Crohn's need to treat recovery as seriously as training.

- Consider **autoregulation**: adjust daily training based on how you feel rather than rigidly following a program
- During mild symptom periods, reduce volume by **30–50%** while maintaining intensity on key lifts
- Never train through significant pain, fever, or signs of a flare

## Nutrition: Fueling Strength While Managing Symptoms

Nutrition is where Crohn's management and strength training intersect most critically. Malabsorption and reduced appetite can make meeting protein needs difficult.

### Evidence-Based Nutrition Strategies

**Protein targets:** Research supports **1.2–1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight** for strength training individuals. For someone weighing 70 kg (154 lbs), that's roughly 84–112 g daily. If whole foods are difficult during symptom periods, consider easily digestible sources like eggs, fish, lean poultry, and—where tolerated—dairy or plant-based protein supplements.

**Timing matters:** Smaller, more frequent meals (4–5 per day) can improve tolerance and absorption for those with digestive challenges.

**Hydration:** Especially important during training and during periods of diarrhea. Electrolyte-containing fluids may help during and after exercise.

**Individualization is non-negotiable:** Foods that trigger symptoms vary widely. Keep a simple food and symptom log to identify patterns. Work with a registered dietitian familiar with IBD—ideally one with sports nutrition experience.

### What to Avoid

- Don't experiment with extreme elimination diets during training blocks; changes should be made one variable at a time
- Avoid high-fiber or high-fat meals immediately before training if they trigger discomfort
- Be cautious with supplements; discuss any new product with your medical team, as some can interact with medications

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

Recovery is where gains are made—and where chronic conditions demand extra attention.

**Sleep:** Aim for **7–9 hours**. Poor sleep is associated with increased inflammation markers and reduced recovery capacity.

**Stress management:** Chronic stress can exacerbate IBD symptoms. Practices like diaphragmatic breathing, light yoga, or short walks support both recovery and symptom management.

**Injury prevention cues:**
- Warm up for **5–10 minutes** with dynamic movements before lifting
- Never sacrifice form for load—especially when fatigued
- Include **unilateral work** (single-leg exercises, single-arm rows) to address imbalances
- Schedule **deload weeks** (reduced volume) every 4–6 weeks

---

## Realistic Timelines

Progress with Crohn's is often **non-linear**. Here's what realistic expectations look like:

- **4–8 weeks:** Improved energy, better movement quality, established routine
- **3–6 months:** Noticeable strength gains (often 20–40% on beginner lifts), improved body composition
- **6–12 months:** Meaningful muscle development, better symptom management patterns, increased confidence
- **Beyond:** Sustainable lifestyle integration

Expect setbacks. Flares, surgeries, or fatigue may interrupt training. The goal isn't a perfect streak—it's returning to the routine after disruptions.

---

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

**No gym? No problem.**
- **Bodyweight circuits** at home: squats, push-ups (incline or knee variants), lunges, planks
- **Resistance bands**: versatile, joint-friendly, and travel-ready—ideal for beginners and those managing fatigue
- **Dumbbells or kettlebells**: a single adjustable set covers most foundational movements
- **Swimming or cycling**: excellent low-impact options during recovery periods or when joint stress is a concern

**When gym access is limited:** Focus on tempo (slow lowering phases), isometric holds, and single-leg variations to increase difficulty without equipment.

---

## Final Thoughts: Strength Is a Process

Craig Collins's transformation wasn't about ignoring his condition—it was about respecting it while refusing to let it define his limits. That balance between ambition and caution is the real lesson for anyone training with a chronic condition.

**Key takeaways:**
- Get medical clearance and build a team (gastroenterologist, dietitian, qualified coach)
- Start conservative, progress gradually, and track how you feel
- Prioritize protein, hydration, sleep, and stress management
- Accept that progress is non-linear—consistency over perfection wins

Strength training won't cure Crohn's disease. But for many people, it can meaningfully improve quality of life, physical capacity, and mental resilience—one careful, well-executed rep at a time.

*Always consult your healthcare provider before beginning a new exercise or nutrition program, especially if you have a chronic medical condition.*
