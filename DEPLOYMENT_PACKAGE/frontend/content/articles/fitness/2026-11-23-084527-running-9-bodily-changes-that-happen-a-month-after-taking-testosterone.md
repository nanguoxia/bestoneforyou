# Testosterone Replacement Therapy and Running: What Actually Changes in the First Month

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) has moved from the fringes of sports medicine into mainstream clinical practice. Between 2002 and 2016, prescriptions for testosterone in the United States tripled, and while the boom has cooled somewhat since, the conversation around TRT—who it's for, what it does, and what it does *not* do—remains intense in gyms, running clubs, and online forums alike.

Much of that conversation focuses on body composition and libido. But for runners and endurance athletes, the first month on TRT raises a different set of questions. Does it change your training? Your recovery? Your hydration needs? And what should you realistically expect—and not expect—in those first four weeks?

This article breaks down the physiological changes commonly reported and documented in the early weeks of testosterone therapy, with practical guidance for runners at every level. A critical caveat up front: **TRT is a prescription therapy for clinically diagnosed hypogonadism, not a performance enhancer for healthy athletes.** In sport, exogenous testosterone is a banned substance under WADA and most governing bodies. Everything below assumes legitimate medical supervision.

---

## The Physiology in Brief

Testosterone influences muscle protein synthesis, red blood cell production, bone density, fat distribution, mood, and libido. When a person with clinically low testosterone begins replacement, serum levels rise—often substantially—within days to weeks, depending on the delivery method (injections peak faster than gels or pellets).

What follows is not a guarantee of any single outcome. Individual response varies widely based on baseline levels, dose, delivery method, age, training history, and overall health. But the first month is generally when some of the earliest measurable shifts occur.

---

## 1. Early Water Retention and Scale Weight

One of the most commonly reported early changes is a modest increase in body weight—typically 1–3 kg (2–6 lbs)—driven largely by fluid retention and, in some cases, increased glycogen storage as training capacity improves.

**For runners:** Don't panic at the scale. A short-term weight bump in weeks two to four is common and usually not fat. If you're training for a race, focus on performance markers (pace at a given heart rate, perceived effort) rather than daily weight.

**Coaching tip:** Weigh yourself at the same time daily, but only interpret *weekly averages*. Single-day fluctuations during the first month on TRT are noise, not signal.

---

## 2. Changes in Energy and Training Motivation

Many patients report improved energy and drive within the first few weeks. This is subjective and partly placebo-prone—but it matters. Feeling more motivated can translate into more consistent training, which is itself a powerful performance lever.

**Practical advice:** Channel early energy gains into *consistency*, not volume spikes. A common mistake is to dramatically increase mileage in week two because you feel great, then land in a fatigue hole by week five. Follow the 10% rule for weekly mileage increases, and keep one easy week for every three build weeks.

**Beginner note:** If you're new to running, use this window to establish a sustainable base—three to four runs per week of 20–40 minutes at a conversational pace. Advanced runners can maintain existing structure but should resist the urge to add intensity prematurely.

---

## 3. Subtle Shifts in Body Composition

Testosterone supports muscle protein synthesis and can reduce fat mass over time—but **one month is too early for meaningful body composition change.** Visible muscle gain typically takes 8–12 weeks or more, and even then, it depends heavily on training stimulus and nutrition.

**What to do:** Treat the first month as a foundation phase. Prioritize protein intake—research consistently supports roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight per day for athletes—and resistance train two to three times per week to give the hormonal signal something to act on. Runners who skip strength work leave much of the potential benefit on the table.

**Equipment-free alternative:** Bodyweight circuits (squats, push-up progressions, split squats, glute bridges, planks) performed 2–3 times weekly, 2–3 sets each, are sufficient to begin.

---

## 4. Possible Increases in Red Blood Cell Mass (and Why Runners Should Care)

Testosterone stimulates erythropoiesis—red blood cell production. Over weeks to months, this can raise hematocrit and hemoglobin, which in theory improves oxygen-carrying capacity. For endurance athletes, this is one reason the hormone is abused as a doping agent.

**The safety consideration:** Elevated hematocrit increases blood viscosity and clot risk. This is a genuine medical concern, not a hypothetical. Guidelines generally recommend monitoring hematocrit at baseline and periodically during therapy, with intervention (dose adjustment, donation, or other medical management) if levels rise excessively.

**Runner-specific note:** If you notice unusual breathlessness, headaches, or a feeling of "thick" fatigue—or if you live and train at altitude, which independently raises hematocrit—raise it with your prescribing clinician. Don't self-manage.

---

## 5. Recovery May Feel Different—But Don't Overinterpret It

Some athletes report faster perceived recovery and less soreness. The evidence for a direct, rapid effect on recovery in the first month is thin, but improved sleep, mood, and energy can indirectly support recovery.

**Actionable habit:** Use a simple readiness check—resting heart rate, sleep quality (1–5), and muscle soreness (1–5)—logged daily. Look at trends over two to three weeks. This gives you objective data instead of vibes.

**Injury prevention:** The most common early-TRT training error in runners is doing too much too soon. Keep strength training form-focused: knees tracking over toes in squats, neutral spine in hinges, controlled tempo (2–3 seconds down) on every rep. Add load gradually—no more than 5–10% per week.

---

## 6. Mood, Sleep, and Libido Shifts

These are among the most consistently reported early changes—and among the most variable. Improved mood and sleep can support training adherence; irritability or sleep disruption can occur, particularly with injection-based protocols that produce peaks and troughs.

**Practical tip:** If you use injections, note how you feel across the dosing cycle. Some people train better in the days following a dose and feel flatter before the next. Adjusting training intensity to match your energy pattern—harder sessions when you feel strong, easier aerobic work when you don't—is a smart, low-risk strategy.

---

## 7. Appetite and Nutrition Changes

Increased appetite is sometimes reported as metabolism and training load rise. Runners should use this as an opportunity to improve fuel quality rather than simply eat more.

**Nutrition guidance:**
- **Protein:** Spread intake across 3–4 meals, roughly 0.3–0.4 g/kg per meal.
- **Carbohydrates:** Match to training—higher on quality session days, moderate on easy days.
- **Hydration:** With possible fluid shifts, drink to thirst and monitor urine color (pale straw is the target).
- **Micronutrients:** Ensure adequate iron, vitamin D, and calcium—especially relevant given bone and blood effects.

---

## 8. Skin, Hair, and Other Androgenic Effects

Acne, oilier skin, and—in some individuals—accelerated hair thinning can appear or worsen in the early months. These are cosmetic but real quality-of-life considerations. Discuss them with your clinician; dose and delivery method can sometimes be adjusted.

---

## 9. What Probably *Won't* Change in a Month

Manage expectations: maximal aerobic capacity (VO₂max), race times, and dramatic physique changes are unlikely to shift meaningfully in four weeks. Fitness is built through training consistency over months. Testosterone—when medically indicated—can support that process, but it does not replace it.

**Realistic timeline:**
- **Weeks 1–4:** Energy, mood, fluid shifts, early subjective changes.
- **Weeks 4–12:** More noticeable body composition and strength trends with consistent training.
- **Months 3–6+:** Fuller physiological adaptation.

---

## Safety and Sport Considerations

- **TRT requires medical diagnosis and monitoring**—baseline and follow-up bloodwork, blood pressure checks, and hematocrit tracking.
- **It is banned in sport.** Competitive runners subject to anti-doping rules must obtain a Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) if medically necessary, and should consult their governing body.
- **Fertility effects** are significant and should be discussed before starting.
- **Cardiovascular risk** remains an area of ongoing research; individual risk factors matter.

---

## The Bottom Line

The first month on clinically indicated testosterone therapy is best viewed as a settling-in period: some early changes in energy, mood, fluid balance, and motivation are common, while major performance or physique transformations are not. For runners, the winning strategy is unchanged—**train consistently, strength train twice weekly, eat enough protein, hydrate well, monitor your body's signals, and keep your clinician in the loop.**

No hormone replaces the fundamentals. But when therapy is appropriate and supervised, it can help you show up to those fundamentals with more energy and better recovery. That's the realistic promise—and it's a meaningful one.
