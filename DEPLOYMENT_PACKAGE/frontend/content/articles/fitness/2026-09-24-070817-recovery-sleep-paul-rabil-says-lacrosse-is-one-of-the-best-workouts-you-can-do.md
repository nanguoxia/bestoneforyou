# Lacrosse Is One of the Best Workouts You Can Do — Here's What Fitness Enthusiasts Can Learn From the Sport's GOAT

Paul Rabil didn't just dominate lacrosse — he helped redefine what a professional athlete's training looks like. As one of the most decorated players in the sport's history and co-founder of the Premier Lacrosse League, Rabil has spent years advocating for lacrosse as a complete fitness solution. And from an exercise science standpoint, he has a point.

Here's the case for lacrosse as a training modality, plus actionable takeaways you can apply whether you play the sport or just want to borrow its best elements.

## Why Lacrosse Checks So Many Fitness Boxes

Lacrosse is a hybrid sport. It blends the repeated sprint demands of soccer, the change-of-direction chaos of basketball, the upper-body involvement of hockey, and the hand-eye coordination of baseball. That combination produces a rare training stimulus.

A landmark study in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* found that collegiate lacrosse players cover roughly 5 to 7 kilometers (3–4.5 miles) per game, with a significant portion of that distance covered at high intensity. Players also perform dozens of explosive accelerations and decelerations, plus repeated jumps, cuts, and rotational movements.

The physiological payoff includes:

- **Improved aerobic capacity (VO2 max):** Game play consistently pushes heart rate into the 80–90% of maximum range during shifts.
- **Enhanced anaerobic power:** Short, maximal-effort bursts train the phosphagen and glycolytic systems.
- **Better agility and reactivity:** Constant cutting and dodging sharpen neuromuscular coordination.
- **Full-body strength endurance:** Shooting, checking, and cradling build shoulder, core, and grip strength.

In short, lacrosse is a time-efficient way to train multiple energy systems and movement patterns in a single session — something most gym routines struggle to replicate.

## What Rabil's Approach Teaches Everyday Athletes

Rabil's longevity at the top of a physically punishing sport didn't come from talent alone. His training philosophy centers on three pillars that apply to anyone chasing fitness goals:

### 1. Build a Broad Base Before You Specialize

Rabil has spoken about spending his offseasons on general physical preparedness — sprinting, lifting, mobility work — rather than only sport-specific drills. For beginners, this means not rushing into advanced programming. A base of 3–4 weeks of general strength and aerobic work pays off before adding intensity.

### 2. Train Rotation and Anti-Rotation

Lacrosse is a rotational sport. Shooting, passing, and dodging all demand core power and stability. Rabil's routines have included medicine ball throws, cable rotations, and anti-rotation holds like Pallof presses.

**Actionable cue:** Keep your ribs stacked over your pelvis during rotational work. Don't let the lower back arch as you twist — drive the movement from your hips and thoracic spine.

### 3. Prioritize Recovery

Rabil has been vocal about sleep, soft tissue work, and off-field recovery as non-negotiables. This aligns with a growing body of evidence: a 2021 review in *Sports Medicine* found that athletes sleeping fewer than 7 hours per night had a 1.7 times higher risk of injury.

## A Sample Lacrosse-Inspired Workout (No Lacrosse Experience Needed)

You can borrow the sport's best qualities without stepping on a field. Try this 30–40 minute circuit 2–3 times per week.

**Warm-up (5–8 minutes)**
- World's greatest stretch: 5 reps per side
- Leg swings: 10 per direction
- Banded shoulder dislocates: 15 reps

**Main Circuit (3 rounds, 45 seconds work / 15 seconds rest)**
1. **Lateral shuffles** — stay low, chest up, push off the outside foot
2. **Medicine ball rotational throws** — 8–10 reps per side, explosive through the hips
3. **Dumbbell push press** — drive from the legs, lock out overhead
4. **Cone or line drills (5-10-5 shuttle)** — plant hard, change direction under control
5. **Plank with shoulder taps** — keep hips level, no rocking

**Cooldown (5 minutes)**
- Hip flexor stretch: 30 seconds per side
- Thoracic rotation: 10 reps per side
- Diaphragmatic breathing: 5 slow breaths

**Beginner modification:** Reduce work intervals to 30 seconds and rounds to 2. Use bodyweight movements before adding load.

**Advanced modification:** Add a fourth round, increase work to 60 seconds, or wear a lightly weighted vest for the shuttle runs.

## Nutrition and Hydration Notes

Lacrosse-style training is metabolically expensive. Two practical guidelines:

- **Pre-session:** Eat a mixed meal 2–3 hours before, or a small carb-focused snack (banana, rice cakes) 30–60 minutes out.
- **Post-session:** Aim for 20–30 grams of protein plus carbohydrates within a couple of hours to support recovery. Chocolate milk remains a well-supported, cost-effective option.

Hydration matters too. A 2019 study in the *International Journal of Sports Nutrition and Exercise Metabolism* found that even 2% body mass dehydration impaired sprint performance and decision-making. Drink 16–20 ounces of water 2 hours before training and sip throughout.

## Injury Prevention and Safety

Lacrosse has a reputation for contact, but most non-contact injuries in the sport — and in lacrosse-style training — stem from poor deceleration mechanics and inadequate hip strength.

- **Master landing mechanics:** When jumping or cutting, land softly with knees tracking over toes.
- **Strengthen the posterior chain:** Glute bridges, Romanian deadlifts, and Nordic hamstring curls reduce hamstring strain risk.
- **Progress gradually:** Add no more than 10% to your weekly training volume to avoid overuse issues.
- **Warm up specifically:** Cold muscles don't tolerate sprinting and cutting well.

If you have a history of ACL injury, ankle instability, or lower back pain, consult a physical therapist before adding high-intensity change-of-direction work.

## Realistic Timelines

- **Weeks 1–4:** Improved coordination, better work capacity, less soreness after sessions.
- **Weeks 4–8:** Noticeable gains in sprint recovery, agility, and core stability.
- **Weeks 8–12:** Measurable improvements in VO2 max and power output if you're training consistently.

Fat loss and visible body composition changes typically take 8–12 weeks with consistent training and nutrition. Muscle and strength gains follow similar or slightly longer timelines.

## The Bottom Line

Paul Rabil's endorsement of lacrosse isn't just hometown pride — it's backed by the sport's unique blend of aerobic, anaerobic, and neuromuscular demands. Whether you pick up a stick or simply steal the training principles, you'll build a more complete, resilient athlete in the process.

Start where you are. Train the patterns, respect recovery, and give it 8–12 weeks. Your fitness — and your future self — will thank you.
