# Fueling the Climb: What Endurance Athletes Like Rémi Bonnet Teach Us About Nutrition for Mountain Running

When Rémi Bonnet powers up a steep alpine gradient, the gear he chooses—shoes, layers, and even the watch on his wrist—gets plenty of attention. But for athletes chasing their own mountain goals, the more instructive story lies beneath the surface: how elite climbers fuel, recover, and protect their bodies on sustained vertical efforts. This article translates those principles into practical nutrition and training guidance you can apply at any level.

## Why Mountain Running Demands a Different Nutritional Approach

Vertical running and skyrunning place unique demands on the body. Sustained climbs at altitude increase energy expenditure dramatically—research on uphill running suggests energy costs can rise 30–50% compared with flat running at the same pace—while reduced oxygen availability and cold exposure add physiological stress. Recent sports nutrition consensus statements (including the 2023 IOC consensus on sports nutrition) emphasize individualized carbohydrate and protein strategies, hydration monitoring, and gut training as cornerstones of endurance performance.

The takeaway for recreational athletes: you don't need an elite's exact plan, but you do need a plan built around duration, intensity, and terrain.

## Nutrition Foundations for Climbing Athletes

### 1. Carbohydrate Timing for Sustained Climbs

- **Under 60 minutes:** Water is generally sufficient. For harder efforts, a small carbohydrate rinse (a few sips of a sports drink) can help perceived effort.
- **1–2.5 hours:** Aim for roughly 30–60 g of carbohydrate per hour from easily digestible sources (gels, chews, bananas, rice cakes).
- **Beyond 2.5 hours:** Evidence supports 60–90 g per hour for trained athletes, ideally from multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose + fructose blends) to improve absorption. Beginners should build toward these numbers gradually.

**Practical tip:** Practice race-day fueling on training climbs. "Gut training"—rehearsing carbohydrate intake during long efforts—reduces GI distress, a common limiter in mountain events.

### 2. Protein for Repair After Vertical Work

Uphill running creates substantial eccentric and concentric muscle loading, especially in the calves and glutes. Post-session, aim for 20–40 g of high-quality protein (whey, dairy, soy, or a mixed meal) within a few hours. Spread 1.2–1.6 g/kg of body weight across the day if you're training frequently. Vegetarian athletes should emphasize leucine-rich combinations—legumes with grains, tofu, or dairy—to support recovery.

### 3. Hydration and Electrolytes at Altitude

Cold mountain air and heavy breathing increase fluid losses that are easy to overlook. A simple self-check: pale-yellow urine and stable body weight before and after sessions. For efforts over 90 minutes, include sodium (roughly 300–600 mg per liter of fluid, adjusted to sweat rate and conditions). Avoid overdrinking—hyponatremia remains a real risk in endurance events.

### 4. Micronutrients Worth Watching

Iron status matters more at altitude; plant-based athletes and menstruating runners should check ferritin periodically. Vitamin D and adequate calcium support bone health, particularly for athletes training on hard surfaces and steep descents.

## Training and Recovery Principles That Pair With Good Nutrition

- **Progress vertical gain gradually:** Beginners should add roughly 10% weekly to climbing volume. Advanced athletes can periodize with dedicated hill blocks, but recovery weeks every 3–4 weeks remain essential.
- **Form cues for climbs:** Shorten stride, run tall with a slight forward lean from the ankles (not the waist), and drive the arms. On very steep grades, power-hiking is often more efficient than running—don't fight the terrain.
- **Descending is training too:** Eccentric loading on downhills builds resilience but also soreness. Introduce downhill volume conservatively to reduce injury risk.
- **Recovery basics:** Sleep (7–9 hours), easy aerobic days, and mobility work for calves and hip flexors. Watch for early signs of overuse—persistent Achilles or knee pain warrants a training review, not more volume.

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

You don't need alpine terrain to train these systems:

- **No mountains?** Use stadium stairs, treadmill incline intervals (8–15%), or parking-garage ramps.
- **No trail access?** Hill repeats on any incline build the same aerobic and muscular qualities.
- **Watch and wearables:** A GPS watch with altitude and heart-rate data can help pace climbs and monitor load—useful, but not essential. Perceived effort and a simple training log work well for beginners.

## Realistic Timelines

- **Beginner:** Expect noticeable climbing fitness improvements in 6–8 weeks with consistent 2–3 weekly hill sessions.
- **Intermediate:** Meaningful race-performance gains typically emerge over 10–16 weeks of structured training.
- **Advanced:** Fine-tuning fueling and pacing often yields the biggest marginal gains across a season—not dramatic fitness jumps.

## Safety Considerations

Always carry water and calories appropriate to duration and remoteness, dress in layers, and check weather and altitude forecasts. If you're new to altitude, allow acclimatization days and monitor for headache, nausea, or unusual breathlessness. Consult a sports physician before starting intense altitude training, especially with cardiovascular conditions.

## The Bottom Line

Elite mountain runners like Rémi Bonnet rely on meticulously practiced fueling, hydration, and recovery—not just gear. Whether you're chasing your first hill repeat or a multi-hour skyrace, the same principles scale down: rehearse your nutrition, build climbing volume gradually, respect recovery, and let consistency—not equipment—drive your progress.
