# Strength Training as a Tool for Mental Wellness: What a Former College Athlete's Story Teaches Us

## When Exercise Isn't Enough

A recent story about a former college athlete's battle with depression offers a valuable lesson for anyone who treats the gym as therapy: sometimes movement alone isn't a cure. He spent years trying to power through his depression, cycling through several traditional oral antidepressants before finding a medication that worked for him.

His experience challenges a common narrative in fitness culture—that training hard enough will fix everything. It won't. But that doesn't mean exercise is irrelevant to mental health. It means we need a more honest, evidence-based conversation about how training, recovery, and professional care fit together.

## What the Science Actually Says

The research on exercise and mood is genuinely encouraging. A 2023 meta-analysis published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* examined 97 reviews covering more than 128,000 participants and found that physical activity was associated with meaningful reductions in depression and anxiety symptoms. Notably, moderate-intensity activity—not maximal effort—showed some of the strongest effects, and shorter, consistent sessions outperformed occasional long ones.

Other findings worth knowing:
- **Resistance training appears particularly effective** for depressive symptoms. A 2018 JAMA Psychiatry meta-analysis of 33 trials found that strength training significantly reduced depressive symptoms, with benefits appearing across all training volumes—even two sessions per week.
- **Timing matters less than consistency.** A 2022 study in *JAMA Psychiatry* found that people who accumulated about 2.5 hours of moderate activity per week had lower depression risk than those who were inactive, regardless of how that time was distributed.
- **Exercise is not a substitute for treatment in moderate-to-severe depression.** Guidelines from major psychiatric bodies consistently position exercise as an adjunct—a complement to therapy and/or medication, not a replacement.

That last point is where the athlete's story becomes instructive rather than discouraging.

## Practical Training Guidance for Mental Wellness

### Beginners
- **Start with 2–3 sessions per week, 20–30 minutes.** Brisk walking, bodyweight circuits, or light dumbbell work are sufficient. The goal in the first month is showing up, not training volume.
- **Use a simple full-body template:** squat pattern (chair sit-to-stands), hinge (hip bridges), push (wall or incline push-ups), pull (band rows), and carry (light farmer walks).
- **Form cues:** keep ribs stacked over hips, brace gently as if bracing for a cough, and exhale on the effort. Stop a set when form degrades, not when you're exhausted.

### Intermediate
- **3–4 sessions per week**, mixing two strength days with one or two aerobic sessions. Aim for 8–12 reps per exercise at a controlled tempo (2 seconds down, 1 second up).
- **Progressive overload should be gradual:** add 2–5% load or one rep per set weekly when all sets feel manageable.
- **Track mood alongside training.** Many lifters notice mood benefits 24–48 hours post-session; logging both helps you identify patterns and realistic expectations.

### Advanced
- **Maintain 4–5 training days** but protect one full rest day. Overtraining—characterized by persistent fatigue, sleep disruption, and irritability—can worsen mood. Watch for resting heart rate creeping up 5–7 beats per minute over baseline.
- **Deload every 4–6 weeks** by reducing volume 40–50%. This is especially important if you're managing depression, since fatigue tolerance may fluctuate.

## Nutrition Considerations

- **Protein:** 1.6–2.2 g per kg of bodyweight daily supports recovery and muscle retention, especially during periods of low motivation when meals may be skipped.
- **Omega-3s:** A 2019 review in *Translational Psychiatry* found EPA-dominant omega-3 supplementation (1–2 g/day) was associated with modest antidepressant effects. Fatty fish twice weekly is a reasonable food-first approach.
- **Blood sugar stability:** Regular meals with fiber, protein, and healthy fats help avoid energy crashes that can mimic or worsen low mood.
- **Caffeine timing:** Avoid caffeine within 8–10 hours of bedtime; poor sleep is a known amplifier of depressive symptoms.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

- **Sleep is non-negotiable.** Adults need 7–9 hours. If sleep is disrupted, reduce training intensity before reducing training frequency—consistency protects mood.
- **Warm up 5–10 minutes** with dynamic movement (leg swings, arm circles, light cardio). Cool down with 5 minutes of easy movement.
- **Pain is a signal, not a challenge.** Sharp joint pain, numbness, or pain that worsens across sessions warrants a professional assessment.
- **Mobility work** (10 minutes, 2–3 times weekly) targeting hips, thoracic spine, and shoulders reduces injury risk without eating into recovery.

## Equipment and Environment Alternatives

- **No gym?** Resistance bands, a pull-up bar, and a pair of adjustable dumbbells cover nearly every movement pattern. Bodyweight progressions (incline push-ups → floor push-ups → decline push-ups) work anywhere.
- **Limited space?** A 2×2-meter area is enough for circuits, carries, and mobility work.
- **Outdoors:** Hill sprints, park circuits, and rucking (weighted walking) combine aerobic and strength benefits with documented mood effects from time in nature.

## Coaching Perspective

From a coaching standpoint, the most common mistake is treating training as a cure-all and ignoring warning signs. Three practical tips:

1. **Set process goals, not outcome goals.** "Train three times this week" is achievable on a bad day; "lose 5 kg" is not.
2. **Have a minimum viable session.** On low days, a 10-minute walk or two sets of squats still counts. Consistency beats intensity.
3. **Build a support team.** A coach, therapist, and physician can work together. Exercise professionals should never advise on medication—and athletes should never self-adjust prescriptions.

## Realistic Timelines

- **Mood benefits:** Often noticeable within 2–4 weeks of consistent activity, with stronger effects at 8–12 weeks.
- **Strength gains:** Beginners can expect 20–40% strength increases in the first 8–12 weeks; advanced lifters progress far more slowly.
- **Body composition changes:** Visible changes typically take 8–12 weeks with consistent training and nutrition.

## The Bottom Line

The former college athlete's story isn't a cautionary tale about exercise failing—it's a reminder that mental health is multifaceted. Training is a powerful, evidence-supported tool, but it works best alongside professional care, adequate sleep, sound nutrition, and honest self-awareness. Build the habit, respect recovery, and don't hesitate to seek help when you need it.
