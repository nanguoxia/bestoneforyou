# The 6 Best Adjustable Kettlebells, Tested by Editors and Certified Trainers

**Our top picks from REP Fitness and Bells of Steel deliver gym-quality performance in a space-saving package—here's what our testing revealed.**

---

If you've ever tried to build a home gym, you know the kettlebell dilemma: a full set of traditional bells can cost upward of $1,000 and eat up an entire corner of your garage. Adjustable kettlebells solve both problems—but only if you pick the right one. A poorly engineered adjustable bell can rattle, shift mid-swing, or worse, fail under load.

To find the models worth your money, our editors and certified trainers spent eight weeks testing six leading adjustable kettlebells across more than 40 workouts—swings, cleans, presses, Turkish get-ups, and carries. We evaluated build quality, weight-switching speed, handle ergonomics, footprint, and long-term durability. Here's what we found.

---

## Why Adjustable Kettlebells Are Worth Considering

A single 35-pound cast-iron kettlebell typically runs $60–$100, and most lifters need at least three or four bells to progress. Adjustable models consolidate that into one unit that fits under a desk. For apartment dwellers, traveling athletes, or anyone on a budget, that's a compelling trade-off.

The trade-off historically? Adjustables have a reputation for feeling "clunky" compared to competition bells. The good news: recent designs have closed much of that gap. Our testing confirmed that the best current models deliver swing feel and balance that rival fixed bells—provided you choose carefully.

**One safety note up front:** Always confirm the locking mechanism is fully seated before loading the bell overhead. We recommend testing each weight setting with a slow, controlled two-handed swing before adding speed or volume.

## Buying Advice: Matching the Bell to Your Goals

**Beginners (0–6 months of training):** Prioritize a lighter range (12–35 lb) and an easy-to-verify locking system. Master the hinge, swing, and goblet squat before chasing load.

**Intermediate lifters:** A 20–50 lb range covers most home programming. Add a second bell later for double-bell work.

**Advanced lifters:** Competition-style adjustables with heavier ranges (up to 70 lb) support sport-specific training and loaded carries.

**Environment matters:** In apartments, choose rubber-based or coated bells to protect floors and reduce noise. If you travel, check that the bell disassembles flat enough for a suitcase—some do, some don't.

---

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

Kettlebell training is deceptively demanding on grip, forearms, and the posterior chain.

- **Grip care:** Limit high-volume swing and carry days to 2–3 per week initially. Forearm soreness that lingers beyond 48 hours signals you need more recovery.
- **Form first:** Hinge at the hips, keep the bell close on the backswing, and let your hips—not your arms—drive the bell. Snap, don't lift.
- **Sleep and protein:** Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep and roughly 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of bodyweight daily to support adaptation, per current sports nutrition consensus.
- **Deload:** Every 4–6 weeks, cut volume by 30–40% for a week. Most nagging overuse issues in kettlebell training trace back to insufficient deloading.
