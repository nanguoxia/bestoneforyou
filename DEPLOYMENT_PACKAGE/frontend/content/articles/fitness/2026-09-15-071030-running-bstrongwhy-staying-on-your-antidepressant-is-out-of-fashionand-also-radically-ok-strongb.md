# The Runner’s High vs. The Med Reset: Why Staying on Antidepressants is Radically OK for Athletes

**Category:** Running / Mental Health
**Reading Time:** 7 Minutes

In the running community, we obsess over the "natural high." We chase the endorphin rush, the clarity that comes after a tempo run, and the profound sense of calm that follows a long Sunday morning effort. There is a pervasive narrative in fitness culture that movement is the ultimate cure-all—a natural substitute for therapy and medication.

But here is the uncomfortable truth: For many athletes, running is not a replacement for mental health treatment. It is a supplement to it. Yet, a dangerous trend has emerged where athletes feel pressured to ditch their antidepressants in favor of "just running it off."

Let’s be clear: Staying on your antidepressant is not a failure of willpower. It is not "cheating" at wellness. In fact, for many, it is the very thing that makes lacing up possible.

## The Myth of the "Overprescribed" Runner

There is a growing cultural narrative that we are living in an era of mass overprescription. While it is true that medication should never be the *only* tool in the toolbox, the idea that athletes are somehow immune to clinical depression or anxiety is scientifically false.

Elite runners, weekend warriors, and casual joggers all share the same neurochemistry. While exercise is a potent tool for managing mild-to-moderate symptoms of depression and anxiety, it is not a cure for clinical major depressive disorder or generalized anxiety disorder.

**The Science Check:**
A 2023 study published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* found that while exercise is highly effective for mild depression, it is not a standalone treatment for severe clinical depression. For those with moderate-to-severe symptoms, a combination of pharmacotherapy (medication) and psychotherapy remains the gold standard.

**The Runner’s Dilemma:**
When you stop taking your medication because you feel "better" (often because the medication is working), you risk a relapse. In the running world, a relapse doesn't just mean feeling sad; it means losing the motivation to train, experiencing chronic fatigue, and losing the joy of movement.

## Why "Just Run" Isn't Always Enough

Running does wonders for the brain. It increases BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor), boosts serotonin, and regulates the HPA axis (your stress response). However, for a brain that struggles to produce or regulate these chemicals on its own, the "natural high" is often not enough to bridge the gap.

Think of it this way: Running builds the engine, but medication often provides the oil that keeps the engine from seizing up.

### The "Fitness Trap"
Many athletes fall into the "Fitness Trap" where they believe that if they just train harder, eat cleaner, and sleep better, they can overcome their mental health struggles. This leads to:
1.  **Overtraining:** Trying to outrun depression leads to injury and burnout.
2.  **Shame:** When running doesn't cure the depression, the athlete feels like a failure.
3.  **Guilt:** Feeling guilty for needing medication when they are "supposed" to be healthy.

## Practical Strategies: Integrating Meds and Miles

If you are an athlete managing mental health, you don't have to choose between the track and the pharmacy. Here is how to optimize both.

### 1. Timing is Everything
Many antidepressants (like SSRIs) can cause initial side effects like drowsiness or nausea. This can impact your workout.
- **Actionable Tip:** Talk to your doctor about the timing of your dose. If your medication makes you drowsy, take it at night. If it gives you energy or nausea, take it after your morning run. Consistency is key for both medication efficacy and training gains.

### 2. The "Start Low, Go Slow" Rule
When starting a new medication, your body needs time to adjust. Your cardiovascular system might feel different.
- **Beginner Advice:** Do not schedule a PR attempt or a race in the first 4–6 weeks of starting a new medication. Stick to Zone 2 (conversational pace) running.
- **Advanced Advice:** Be aware that some antidepressants can affect thermoregulation (sweating). You may overheat faster. Hydrate more than usual and monitor your heart rate rather than just your pace.

### 3. Nutrition for Brain and Body
Medication and running both demand specific nutrients.
- **Omega-3s:** Research suggests Omega-3 fatty acids can support antidepressant efficacy. Incorporate salmon, walnuts, or algae oil supplements.
- **Hydration:** Dry mouth is a common side effect of antidepressants. This can lead to dehydration and cramping. Increase your water intake and monitor electrolyte levels.
- **Protein:** Ensure adequate protein intake (1.6g/kg of body weight) to support recovery and neurotransmitter production.

### 4. Communication with Your Care Team
Your psychiatrist and your running coach (or physical therapist) should ideally be on the same page.
- **Actionable Tip:** If you are experiencing persistent fatigue or unusual muscle soreness, don't just assume it's training. It could be a medication side effect. Report it to your doctor immediately. There are many different classes of antidepressants; finding the right one is a process of trial and error, not a life sentence.

## Safety Considerations for the Medicated Runner

- **Heat Sensitivity:** Some antidepressants (especially TCAs and SSRIs) can impair the body's ability to cool down. In hot weather, run indoors, early in the morning, or on a treadmill.
- **Heart Rate Variability (HRV):** Medication can alter your resting heart rate. Don't panic if your RHR increases slightly. Focus on Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) rather than strict heart rate zones during the adjustment period.
- **Bone Density:** Some medications (specifically SSRIs) have been linked to a slight decrease in bone mineral density in long-term users. Ensure you are doing weight-bearing exercises and getting adequate Calcium and Vitamin D.

## Realistic Timelines: What to Expect

- **Weeks 1-2:** Side effects may be present (nausea, headache). Keep running easy or take rest days. Do not judge your fitness based on this period.
- **Weeks 4-8:** Therapeutic effects often kick in. You may notice a return of motivation to run. This is a great time to establish a consistent routine.
- **Months 3-6:** Full effects. You should feel more stable. This is when you can start incorporating harder workouts (intervals, tempo runs) if your body feels ready.

## The Verdict: Radically OK

Is running a powerful tool for mental health? Absolutely. It reduces cortisol, boosts mood, and provides a sense of accomplishment.

But is it a substitute for prescribed medication when you have a clinical condition? No.

Staying on your antidepressant while pursuing your running goals is not out of fashion. It is a sign of maturity, self-awareness, and radical self-care. It allows you to be the athlete you want to be—not just the person fighting a chemical imbalance.

So, take your meds, lace up your shoes, and hit the pavement. Your brain—and your body—will thank you for both.

### Quick Reference Guide

| Scenario | Recommendation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Starting a new med** | Reduce training volume by 20-30% for 2 weeks. |
| **Feeling drowsy** | Take meds before bed. Run in the morning if possible. |
| **Feeling nauseous** | Take meds with food. Avoid high-intensity intervals immediately after dosing. |
| **Hot weather** | Run indoors or hydrate aggressively. Meds can affect sweating. |
| **Missed a dose** | Do not double up. Consult your pharmacist regarding your specific medication. |
