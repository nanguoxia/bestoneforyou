# Lacrosse Is One of the Best Workouts You Can Do — Here's What Fitness Enthusiasts Can Steal From the Sport

Paul Rabil didn't just dominate lacrosse — he helped redefine what a professional athlete's training looks like. As one of the most decorated players in the sport's history and co-founder of the Premier Lacrosse League, Rabil has spent years arguing that lacrosse deserves a place alongside running, swimming, and basketball as one of the most complete workouts a person can do.

He has a point. And whether you ever pick up a stick or not, the physical demands of lacrosse offer a blueprint for building a more athletic, resilient body — one that can sprint, cut, throw, absorb contact, and recover, all in the same session.

Here's what the sport actually demands of your body, what the science says about that kind of training, and how to apply it to your own fitness routine, whatever your level.

---

## Why Lacrosse Is a Genuinely Complete Workout

Lacrosse is often described as a hybrid of soccer, basketball, and hockey — and that combination shows up in the physiology.

A typical game or high-intensity practice involves:

- **Repeated sprint efforts** with short recovery windows
- **Rapid directional changes** and decelerations
- **Upper-body rotational power** for passing and shooting
- **Full-body contact and jostling** that demand core and grip strength
- **Sustained aerobic work** across four quarters or a full field session

Research on team-sport athletes consistently shows that this mix of high-intensity intervals and multidirectional movement produces broad adaptations: improved VO2 max, better change-of-direction speed, and greater muscular endurance than steady-state cardio alone.

In other words, lacrosse trains the qualities that most gym programs train separately — often in a single 60-minute session.

---

## The Four Pillars of Lacrosse Fitness (and How to Train Them)

### 1. Sprint Capacity and Repeatability

Lacrosse is a stop-and-go sport. Players may sprint 20–40 yards, recover for a few seconds, then go again — dozens of times per game.

**How to train it:**
- **Beginners:** Start with 10 x 20-yard strides at about 80% effort, walking back to the start between reps. Do this once or twice a week.
- **Intermediate:** Progress to 8 x 40 yards at 90% effort with 30–45 seconds rest.
- **Advanced:** Try repeat-sprint sets — 6 x 60 yards with 20 seconds rest, two to three sets.

**Form cue:** Stay tall through the torso, drive the arms front-to-back (not across the body), and land under your hips rather than reaching forward.

**Timeline:** Expect noticeable improvements in repeat-sprint ability within 4–6 weeks of consistent work.

### 2. Change of Direction and Deceleration

Cutting is where most non-contact injuries happen — especially at the knee and ankle. Lacrosse players decelerate and re-accelerate constantly, which places high demands on the quads, glutes, and adductors.

**How to train it:**
- **Beginner:** Lateral shuffles, cone weaves, and simple 5-10-5 drills at moderate speed.
- **Intermediate:** Add resisted lateral band walks and split-stance deceleration drills.
- **Advanced:** Reactive cutting drills with a partner or visual cue, plus single-leg landing progressions.

**Safety cue:** Keep your knee tracking over your second toe on cuts. If your knee collapses inward, reduce speed and rebuild control before adding intensity.

**Coach's tip:** Most athletes train acceleration far more than deceleration. Spend equal time learning to *stop* well — it's where games are won and injuries are prevented.

### 3. Rotational Power and Upper-Body Strength

Shooting and passing generate force from the ground up, transferring through the hips, core, and shoulders. That's why lacrosse players often have well-developed rotational strength.

**How to train it:**
- **Beginner:** Medicine ball chest passes and rotational throws against a wall.
- **Intermediate:** Cable or band woodchops, Pallof presses, and half-kneeling rotations.
- **Advanced:** Explosive med ball slams, rotational throws, and sport-specific shooting drills.

**Form cue:** Rotate through the hips and thoracic spine — not just the lower back. If you feel pinching in your lumbar spine, you're compensating.

### 4. Aerobic Base and Recovery Capacity

All that sprinting only works if you have the engine to recover between efforts. A solid aerobic base speeds recovery between plays and between workouts.

**How to train it:**
- **Beginners:** 20–30 minutes of easy jogging, cycling, or rowing 2–3 times per week.
- **Intermediate:** Add one tempo run or interval session per week.
- **Advanced:** Periodize with one long easy session and one high-intensity interval session weekly.

**Recovery note:** Sleep is the single most powerful recovery tool available. Aim for 7–9 hours, and treat it as part of training, not separate from it.

## Nutrition for Stop-and-Go Athletes

Lacrosse-style training burns significant energy and places a premium on quick recovery between efforts and between sessions.

**Before training (1–2 hours out):**
- 30–60 g of carbohydrates plus 15–25 g of protein
- Example: oatmeal with whey protein and a banana, or Greek yogurt with granola

**During long sessions (60+ minutes):**
- 30–60 g of carbs per hour from sports drink, gels, or easily digestible snacks
- Sip water to thirst; add electrolytes if you're sweating heavily

**After training (within 1–2 hours):**
- 20–40 g of protein plus 0.5–1 g of carbs per kilogram of body weight
- Example: chicken and rice bowl, or a protein shake with fruit

**Hydration:** Even 2% dehydration can impair sprint performance and reaction time. Weigh yourself before and after hard sessions — replace 16–24 oz of fluid for every pound lost.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention

Lacrosse-style training is demanding, and the most common setbacks are hamstring strains, ankle sprains, and knee injuries. Most are preventable with smart programming.

**Key strategies:**
- **Warm up dynamically** — leg swings, lunges with rotation, skips, and progressive sprint buildups. Skip static stretching before intense work.
- **Strengthen the posterior chain** — hamstrings, glutes, and calves. Nordic hamstring curls and single-leg Romanian deadlifts are excellent.
- **Train single-leg stability** — most cutting and landing happens on one leg.
- **Respect deload weeks** — every 3–4 weeks, reduce volume by 30–40% to let connective tissue recover.
- **Sleep and manage stress** — under-recovery shows up as slower sprint times, poor mood, and higher injury risk.

**Realistic timeline:** Beginners can expect meaningful fitness gains in 6–8 weeks. Intermediate athletes often see performance improvements in 4–6 weeks with consistent, progressive training. Advanced athletes should plan in 8–12 week blocks with clear phases.

## No Team? No Problem — Adapting Lacrosse Training Anywhere

You don't need a field, a stick, or a team to benefit from this style of training.

**Minimal equipment (home or park):**
- Sprint intervals on a sidewalk or grass strip
- Cone or shoe-marker agility drills
- Bodyweight rotational work and med ball throws if you have one

**Gym-based alternative:**
- Treadmill sprint intervals
- Cable rotational work
- Sled pushes or bike sprints for repeat-effort conditioning

**Small-space option:**
- Lateral band walks, split squats, and shadow cutting drills in a hallway or living room

**Coaching perspective:** The best program is the one you'll actually do. Start with two sprint/agility sessions and two strength sessions per week, and build from there.

---

## The Bottom Line

Paul Rabil's case for lacrosse isn't just sport promotion — it's a case for training like an athlete. Sprint, cut, rotate, recover, repeat. That formula builds a body that's fast, strong, durable, and ready for almost anything.

You don't need to play lacrosse to train like a lacrosse player. You just need to borrow the principles: move in multiple directions, train power and endurance together, fuel your work, and treat recovery as seriously as your hardest session.

Start where you are. Progress gradually. And give your body the time it needs to adapt — because the athletes who last are the ones who train smart, not just hard.
