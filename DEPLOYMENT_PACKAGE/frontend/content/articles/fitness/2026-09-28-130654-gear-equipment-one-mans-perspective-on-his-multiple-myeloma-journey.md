# Strength Training Through Multiple Myeloma: What One Patient's Journey Teaches Us About Resilience, Recovery, and Smart Training

When most people think about a multiple myeloma diagnosis, they picture hospital rooms, chemotherapy infusions, and an uncertain future. What they rarely picture is a man in his sixties, carefully loading a barbell, tracking his protein intake, and texting his training partner about next week's squat session.

But that's exactly the reality for a growing number of patients—and it's a reality that holds powerful lessons for anyone chasing fitness goals, whether you're a beginner lacing up your shoes for the first time or a seasoned lifter with decades under the bar.

Multiple myeloma is a blood cancer that forms in plasma cells, the immune system's antibody factories. It's relatively rare—the American Cancer Society estimates roughly 35,000 new cases in the United States each year—but it's also one of the most treatable blood cancers when caught early. And increasingly, exercise is being recognized not as an afterthought in cancer care, but as a core part of the treatment plan.

Here's what that means for your training, your nutrition, and your long game—whether you're a patient, a survivor, or simply someone who wants to build a body and mind resilient enough to handle whatever life throws at you.

## Why Exercise Matters More Than Ever in Cancer Care

For decades, the standard advice for cancer patients was rest. Conserve energy. Don't push yourself. That advice has been thoroughly overturned by modern exercise science.

A landmark body of research—including studies published in *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* and the *Journal of Clinical Oncology*—now shows that structured exercise during and after cancer treatment can:

- Reduce cancer-related fatigue by 30–50% (one of the most debilitating side effects of treatment)
- Improve physical function and quality of life
- Reduce anxiety and depression symptoms
- Preserve muscle mass and bone density, which are especially critical in myeloma because the disease itself can cause bone lesions
- Potentially improve treatment tolerance and completion rates

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) now recommends that cancer survivors and patients aim for the same general guidelines as the general population: at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, plus two or more sessions of resistance training.

For myeloma patients specifically, resistance training is not optional—it's protective. Myeloma frequently causes bone damage, and weight-bearing exercise helps stimulate bone remodeling and maintain skeletal strength. That said, it must be approached intelligently.

## The Patient's Perspective: What One Man's Journey Reveals

The original story behind this article is simple but profound: a man living with multiple myeloma refused to let the diagnosis define his limits. His journey wasn't about heroics—it was about consistency, adaptation, and hope.

What stands out in his account is not that he trained hard every day. It's that he trained *smart*. He listened to his body. He adjusted when treatment weeks were rough. He celebrated small wins—a few extra reps, a slightly heavier weight, a walk around the block when fatigue threatened to keep him on the couch.

That's a lesson for all of us. Fitness isn't about smashing PRs every session. It's about showing up, adapting, and building a body that can handle the demands of life—including the hard parts.

## Practical Training Advice for Cancer Patients and Survivors

If you're navigating cancer treatment or recovery, here's how to approach training safely and effectively. Always clear any exercise program with your oncology team first, especially if you have bone lesions, low blood counts, or are on medications that affect balance or heart rate.

### For Beginners (Little or No Training Experience)

- **Start with walking.** Aim for 10–20 minutes daily, even if it's just around the house. Walking is low-impact, supports bone health, and fights fatigue.
- **Add light resistance.** Resistance bands or 1–5 lb dumbbells are enough to start. Focus on major movement patterns: sit-to-stand (squats), wall push-ups, and seated rows.
- **Frequency over intensity.** Two to three short sessions per week is plenty. Ten minutes done consistently beats an hour done once.
- **Form cues:** Keep your spine neutral, brace your core gently, and breathe out on exertion. Never hold your breath—this spikes blood pressure and can be dangerous if you're anemic.

### For Intermediate Trainees (Some Experience)

- **Use a 2-day full-body split.** One session can be machine-based, one free-weight based. This reduces joint stress while maintaining muscle.
- **Rep ranges:** 8–12 reps at a moderate effort (RPE 5–7 out of 10). You should feel challenged but not exhausted.
- **Include balance work.** Cancer treatment can affect neuropathy (nerve damage) in the feet. Single-leg stands and heel-to-toe walks help reduce fall risk.
- **Monitor recovery.** If fatigue lasts more than 24 hours after a session, dial back the volume next time.

### For Advanced Trainees (Experienced Lifters)

- **Maintain, don't maximize.** Your goal during treatment is preservation, not PRs. Keep intensity moderate and volume manageable.
- **Avoid heavy axial loading** (barbell back squats, heavy deadlifts) if you have known bone lesions in the spine or hips. Swap in trap bar deadlifts, belt squats, or leg press.
- **Track everything.** Log your workouts, fatigue, sleep, and symptoms. This data helps you and your medical team spot patterns.
- **Deload every 4–6 weeks.** Even if you feel great, planned recovery weeks prevent burnout and injury.

## Nutrition That Supports Treatment and Training

Nutrition during cancer treatment is a moving target. Appetite, taste changes, nausea, and mouth sores can make eating a challenge. But the principles remain:

- **Protein is non-negotiable.** Aim for 1.2–2.0 grams per kilogram of body weight daily to preserve muscle. If you weigh 70 kg (154 lb), that's 84–140 g of protein. Spread it across 3–5 meals. Good sources: eggs, Greek yogurt, chicken, fish, tofu, and protein shakes if appetite is low.
- **Calcium and vitamin D.** Myeloma can weaken bones, so adequate calcium (1,000–1,200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800–1,000 IU/day, or as directed by your doctor) are essential. Dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens, and fatty fish are your friends.
- **Hydration.** Treatment can cause dehydration, especially with diarrhea or vomiting. Aim for pale-yellow urine. Add electrolytes if you're sweating heavily.
- **Anti-inflammatory fats.** Omega-3s from salmon, walnuts, and flaxseed may help with inflammation. Discuss fish oil supplements with your oncologist—they can interact with blood thinners.
- **Small, frequent meals.** If you can't eat a full plate, graze. A handful of nuts, a hard-boiled egg, and a smoothie can add up.

## Recovery and Injury Prevention: The Non-Negotiables

Recovery isn't optional—it's where the adaptation happens. For cancer patients and survivors, it's also where the body fights hardest.

- **Sleep 7–9 hours.** This is when muscle repair and immune function peak. If treatment disrupts your sleep, talk to your doctor about strategies.
- **Listen to your body's signals.** Bone pain, unusual shortness of breath, dizziness, or chest pain means stop and call your doctor. Don't push through.
- **Avoid infection risks.** If your white blood cell count is low, skip crowded gyms. Train at home, outdoors, or during off-peak hours. Wipe down equipment and wash your hands frequently.
- **Manage neuropathy.** If you have numbness or tingling in your feet, avoid unstable surfaces like Bosu balls and prioritize seated or supported exercises.
- **Work with a professional.** A physical therapist or certified cancer exercise specialist (look for ACSM-CET or CETI credentials) can tailor a program to your specific needs.

## Realistic Timelines: What Progress Actually Looks Like

One of the most frustrating parts of any health journey is waiting for results. Here's the honest truth:

- **Weeks 1–4:** You'll likely notice improved mood, better sleep, and slightly less fatigue. Strength gains are mostly neurological—your brain gets better at recruiting muscle.
- **Weeks 4–12:** Visible muscle tone may begin to appear if you're eating enough protein. Daily activities feel easier.
- **Months 3–6:** Meaningful strength gains (10–30% on key lifts) are realistic for beginners. Bone density changes take longer—6–12 months of consistent weight-bearing exercise.
- **Beyond 6 months:** This is where the magic happens. Consistent training becomes part of your identity. Your body adapts, your confidence grows, and your ability to handle treatment side effects often improves.

Remember: progress is not linear. Treatment cycles, infections, and fatigue will create ups and downs. That's normal. The goal is to keep showing up, even if it's just a 10-minute walk.

## Alternatives for Different Equipment and Environments

You don't need a fancy gym. Here's how to train anywhere:

- **Bodyweight only:** Squats, push-ups (wall, incline, or floor), lunges, glute bridges, planks, and bird dogs.
- **Resistance bands:** Great for rows, presses, and lateral walks. Packable and joint-friendly.
- **Dumbbells:** Adjustable sets save space. If you don't have any, water bottles or canned goods work in a pinch.
- **Machines:** Ideal if balance is a concern. Leg press, chest press, and seated row are safe, effective choices.
- **Outdoors:** Walking, hiking, and swimming are excellent low-impact options. Swimming is especially good if you have joint pain or bone lesions—just avoid diving.

## Coaching Wisdom: Tips That Make a Difference

From coaches who work with cancer patients and survivors:

- **Set process goals, not outcome goals.** "Walk 10 minutes today" beats "lose 20 pounds."
- **Use the 2-minute rule.** If you don't feel like training, commit to just 2 minutes. You'll often keep going.
- **Celebrate non-scale victories.** Better sleep, less pain, more energy—these matter more than the number on the scale.
- **Build a support team.** A training partner, a supportive oncologist, a registered dietitian, and a therapist can all play a role.
- **Be patient with yourself.** Healing takes time. Fitness is a long game.

## The Bottom Line

Multiple myeloma is a serious diagnosis, but it is not a death sentence—and it doesn't have to be the end of your fitness journey. As one patient's story shows, hope, resilience, and smart training can coexist with cancer treatment.

Whether you're a patient, a survivor, or simply someone who wants to build a stronger, more resilient body, the principles are the same: move often, lift intelligently, eat enough protein, sleep well, and adapt when life throws curveballs.

Your body is capable of remarkable things. Treat it with respect, train it with purpose, and give it the recovery it needs. The journey may not be straightforward—but it's yours to shape.

**Always consult your healthcare provider before starting or modifying an exercise program, especially if you are undergoing cancer treatment.**
